It hurts almost everywhere, and it has for months. You are exhausted even after a full night in bed. Your thinking feels foggy. Tests come back normal, and someone suggests exercise, which can sound like the last thing your body can handle.

That reaction is understandable. But for fibromyalgia, exercise is not a throwaway suggestion. It is the treatment with the strongest backing in European guidelines, and the trials behind it are worth knowing about, along with their limits [[5]](#source-5). Here is what the research says, and how to start in a way your body can tolerate.

## What fibromyalgia is

Fibromyalgia is a long-lasting condition that causes pain and tenderness throughout the body, along with fatigue and trouble sleeping [[1]](#source-1). Other common symptoms include [[1]](#source-1):

- Stiffness in the muscles and joints
- Numbness or tingling
- Problems with memory and concentration, often called "fibro fog"
- Sensitivity to light, noise, smells and temperature
- Digestive problems

The cause is not known. People with fibromyalgia have a heightened sensitivity to pain, and brain imaging suggests that the way the nervous system processes pain signals is altered [[1]](#source-1). It is not caused by inflammation in the tissues, which is one reason anti-inflammatory pain medicines usually do not help much [[4]](#source-4).

## Who gets it

Anyone can get fibromyalgia, but women are about twice as likely to have it [[2]](#source-2). It usually starts in middle age, tends to run in families and is more common in people with other conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, ankylosing spondylitis, osteoarthritis, chronic back pain, irritable bowel syndrome, depression or anxiety [[1]](#source-1). A review of studies around the world estimated that it affects about 1.8% of the general population [[3]](#source-3).

There are no lab or imaging tests for it. A doctor makes the diagnosis based on widespread pain and the other symptoms, after a history and exam to rule out conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or lupus [[4]](#source-4).

## Why guidelines put exercise first

A panel from the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) reviewed 107 systematic reviews and meta-analyses of fibromyalgia treatments. Exercise was the only therapy that earned a "strong for" recommendation [[5]](#source-5). The panel suggested starting with education and non-drug treatments. If those are not enough, the next steps are tailored to the person: psychological therapies for low mood or unhelpful coping, medicines for severe pain or sleep problems, or a multidisciplinary rehab program for severe disability [[5]](#source-5).

The panel was also honest that the size of the effect for most treatments, exercise included, is modest [[5]](#source-5). The NIH puts it this way: regular exercise is one of the most useful ways to combat fibromyalgia [[4]](#source-4).

## What the trials show

| Approach | What the evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| Aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, swimming) | Small to moderate improvements in quality of life, pain and physical function, based on low- to moderate-quality evidence [[6]](#source-6) |
| Strength training | Improved function, pain, tenderness and strength in women, based on low-quality evidence [[7]](#source-7) |
| Mixed programs (aerobic, strength and stretching) | Probably improve quality of life, physical function and fatigue, though gains may be small for some people [[8]](#source-8) |
| Tai chi | In one large trial, as good as or better than aerobic exercise [[9]](#source-9) |
| Cognitive behavioral therapy | Low-certainty evidence of meaningful gains in mobility and quality of life [[10]](#source-10) |

### Aerobic exercise

A Cochrane review of 13 trials found that aerobic training, done for 6 to 24 weeks, improved several symptoms compared with no exercise [[6]](#source-6). On 0 to 100 scales, the absolute improvements were:

Those gains are modest, and the evidence for pain and function was low quality [[6]](#source-6). Dropout rates were similar with and without exercise, which suggests most people tolerated it, but few trials tracked side effects, so the authors could not be sure about safety [[6]](#source-6).

### Strength training

Lifting weights may sound risky when everything hurts. A Cochrane review of five small trials, all in women, found that moderate to high-intensity strength training for 16 to 21 weeks improved overall function, pain, tender points and leg strength compared with no exercise, with no serious side effects reported [[7]](#source-7). In head-to-head comparisons, aerobic exercise relieved pain a little more, and strength training did better than stretching alone [[7]](#source-7). The evidence was low quality because the trials were few and small [[7]](#source-7).

### Mixing it up

Many programs combine aerobic, strength and flexibility work. A Cochrane review of 29 trials with 2,088 people, 98% of them women, found that mixed exercise probably improves quality of life, physical function and fatigue, and may reduce pain, compared with no exercise [[8]](#source-8). No injuries were reported, but some people had a temporary rise in pain, soreness or tiredness during or after exercise [[8]](#source-8).

### Tai chi

A US trial randomly assigned 226 adults with fibromyalgia to supervised aerobic exercise twice a week for 24 weeks or to one of four tai chi programs [[9]](#source-9). Symptoms improved in every group. Overall, the tai chi groups improved somewhat more than the aerobic group. When the two were given at the same dose, twice a week for 24 weeks, tai chi improved scores on a 100-point fibromyalgia questionnaire by 16.2 points more [[9]](#source-9). Six months of tai chi beat three months, and people attended tai chi classes more often than aerobic sessions. No serious side effects were linked to either program [[9]](#source-9).

### Beyond exercise

An overview of 10 Cochrane reviews covering 181 trials found low-certainty evidence that aerobic and mixed exercise, as well as cognitive behavioral therapy, made a meaningful difference in mobility and, for some, in quality of life [[10]](#source-10). Medicines can also help some people. Certain antidepressants and anti-seizure medicines may ease pain and improve sleep, and it can take some trial and error to find the right one [[4]](#source-4).

## How to start without overdoing it

The biggest mistake is doing too much on a good day and paying for it for a week. A few principles help:

- **Start low and build gradually.** Begin at a level that feels easy, and increase a little at a time [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4).
- **Choose low-impact options.** Walking, cycling, swimming and water exercise are good places to start, and yoga and tai chi are options too [[4]](#source-4).
- **Pace yourself through the day,** so you do not use up all your energy at once [[4]](#source-4).
- **Expect some soreness.** In exercise trials, some people had a temporary rise in pain, soreness or tiredness during or after exercise [[8]](#source-8). If it lasts, scale back and build up more slowly.
- **Protect your sleep.** Keep regular sleep and wake times, keep the bedroom dark, quiet and cool, and limit caffeine, nicotine and alcohol [[4]](#source-4).
- **Check with your doctor** before starting a new exercise routine [[4]](#source-4).

A physical therapist can prescribe a program that fits your current level, adjust it as you go and offer ongoing support [[4]](#source-4). Finding a program you enjoy and can stick with matters too. In the tai chi trial, people attended tai chi classes more often than aerobic sessions [[9]](#source-9).

## When to see a clinician

**Get evaluated** if you have widespread pain, fatigue or sleep problems that last for months, so other conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or lupus can be ruled out [[4]](#source-4).

**See a mental health professional** if low mood, anxiety or stress become hard to manage. These often go hand in hand with fibromyalgia, and treating them can help [[1]](#source-1) [[4]](#source-4).

## The takeaway

- Fibromyalgia is a real condition of heightened pain sensitivity that causes widespread pain, fatigue and sleep problems. It is not caused by inflammation in the tissues.
- Exercise is the only treatment with a strong recommendation in European guidelines.
- Aerobic, strength, mixed and tai chi programs have all improved symptoms in trials. The gains are usually modest, and the evidence is mostly low to moderate quality.
- Start low, build gradually and pace yourself. If soreness after exercise lingers, scale back and build up more slowly.
- Exercise works best as part of a plan that can include education, sleep habits, psychological therapy and, for some, medicine.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If you live with fibromyalgia, an evaluation can find a starting point your body can handle and build a gradual exercise plan you can keep up, with adjustments for the harder days.
