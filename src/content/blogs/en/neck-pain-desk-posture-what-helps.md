Neck pain is one of the most common aches adults deal with. In a national survey, **15.7% of US adults** said they had neck pain lasting a whole day or more in the previous three months, and women reported it more often than men (17.2% vs. 14.0%) [[1]](#source-1). Worldwide, neck pain affected an estimated **203 million people in 2020**, a number projected to reach **269 million by 2050**, mainly because the population is growing and aging [[2]](#source-2).

If your neck aches after long days at a desk or on your phone, here is what the research says, starting with a common belief that does not hold up well.

## Is "bad posture" really the cause?

Many people are told their neck hurts because they slouch or look down at their phone. The research is less clear than that advice suggests:

- In a study of more than 1,100 seventeen-year-olds in Australia, teens were grouped by how they sat. There was **no significant difference in neck pain or headaches** between the posture groups [[3]](#source-3).
- When almost 700 of them were followed up at age 22, posture at 17 did **not** predict persistent neck pain in young men. In young women, the more relaxed, slumped postures were actually linked to **lower** odds of persistent neck pain than sitting upright [[4]](#source-4). The strongest predictor was having neck pain already at 17 [[4]](#source-4).
- A study of 150 young adults in Brazil found **no association between "text neck" posture and neck pain** [[5]](#source-5).

These studies have limits. They looked mostly at young people, and some were cross-sectional. But they suggest that forcing yourself into one "perfect" posture is unlikely to be the answer. A more useful idea, which the NHS also recommends, is to **avoid keeping your neck in the same position for a long time**, for example by changing position and taking short breaks during desk work [[10]](#source-10).

## Strengthening helps; stretching alone does not seem to

A Cochrane review of exercise for neck pain found **moderate-quality evidence** that strength training for the neck, shoulder blade and arm muscles reduces chronic neck pain, and that strengthening and endurance exercise help with neck-related headaches [[6]](#source-6). The same review found that when **only stretching** was used, no benefit should be expected [[6]](#source-6). It noted that there is still uncertainty about the best dose [[6]](#source-6).

Two Danish trials in workers with neck and shoulder pain show how this looks in practice:

- In 48 women with neck muscle pain from repetitive work, including computer work, **specific neck and shoulder strength training** cut their worst pain by about **79%** over 10 weeks, while general fitness training on a bike gave only a short-lived drop [[7]](#source-7).
- In 198 working adults with frequent neck and shoulder pain, **just 2 minutes a day** of progressive resistance training with an elastic band, 5 days a week for 10 weeks, reduced pain in a clinically meaningful way [[8]](#source-8):

| Pain change vs. a control group (0 to 10 scale) | Result |
| --- | --- |
| 2 minutes of training a day | 1.4 points less pain |
| 12 minutes of training a day | 1.9 points less pain |

*Source: Andersen et al., Pain, 2011 [[8]](#source-8). The control group received weekly health information.*

## What physical therapy guidelines recommend

The American Physical Therapy Association’s neck pain guideline sorts neck pain into types. For neck pain with stiffness and limited movement, the type most people with desk-related pain recognize, it recommends [[9]](#source-9):

- **Early on:** manipulation of the upper back (thoracic spine), neck range-of-motion exercises, and strengthening of the shoulder blade and arm muscles
- **After 6 weeks:** endurance exercises for the neck and shoulder girdle
- **If pain lasts more than 12 weeks:** a combined approach with hands-on treatment of the neck and upper back plus a mix of strengthening, endurance, coordination, stretching and aerobic exercise, along with education that promotes an active lifestyle

For neck pain that causes headaches, the guideline recommends supervised mobility exercise early on and, for longer-lasting cases, hands-on treatment combined with neck and shoulder stretching, strengthening and endurance exercise [[9]](#source-9). It also states that without red-flag signs, **no imaging is indicated** [[9]](#source-9), and that clinicians should screen every patient for signs of a more serious problem and refer when needed [[9]](#source-9).

The NHS adds simple advice: **do not wear a neck collar**, because it is better to keep your neck moving, and try gentle flexibility exercises [[10]](#source-10).

## When to get medical help

**Call 911 or go to the emergency room** if you have [[11]](#source-11):

- Fever and headache with a neck so stiff you cannot touch your chin to your chest, which may be a sign of meningitis
- Symptoms of a heart attack, such as shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, vomiting, or arm or jaw pain
- Neck pain after a fall, blow or injury, and you cannot move your arm or hand

Contact your doctor if [[11]](#source-11):

- You have numbness, tingling or weakness in your arm or hand
- You have trouble walking or with your balance, or lose control of your bladder or bowels
- You have swollen glands or a lump in your neck, or trouble swallowing or breathing
- The pain is worse when you lie down or wakes you up at night
- Symptoms do not improve after a week of self-care

## The takeaway

- Neck pain is common, especially in midlife.
- Posture alone is a weaker explanation than many people think; moving and changing position often matters more than holding a "perfect" posture.
- Strengthening the neck and shoulder muscles helps, even in small daily doses; stretching alone does not seem to.
- Physical therapy combines exercise with hands-on care and education, and screens for the rare cases that need a doctor.
