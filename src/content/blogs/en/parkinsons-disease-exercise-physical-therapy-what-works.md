Your hand shakes when it is resting on your lap. Buttons take longer. Your steps have gotten shorter, and turning around in the kitchen feels less steady than it used to. A diagnosis of Parkinson's disease brings a lot of questions, and one of the most common is what you can do yourself.

Exercise is one of the strongest answers. Alongside the care from your doctor, a large body of trials now shows that staying active, and training in the right ways, can improve movement, balance and quality of life [[4]](#source-4) [[5]](#source-5). Here is what that research shows and how to put it to work.

## What Parkinson's disease is

Parkinson's disease is a progressive neurologic disorder [[6]](#source-6). Its three main symptoms are shaking (tremor) of parts of the body, slow movement, and stiff, inflexible muscles [[1]](#source-1). Many people also have balance problems that raise the risk of falls, low mood and anxiety, a reduced sense of smell, trouble sleeping and memory problems [[1]](#source-1).

Most people start to have symptoms after age 50, although some notice them before 40, and men are slightly more likely to develop it than women [[1]](#source-1). In North America, Parkinson's is the second most common age-related neurodegenerative condition. A study of five large cohorts estimated that every year between 108 and 212 of every 100,000 people aged 65 and older are diagnosed with it, with rates rising with age and higher in men [[2]](#source-2).

## Why exercise matters

The American Physical Therapy Association's guideline for Parkinson's, based on systematic reviews of the evidence, gives a strong recommendation to most of what a good program includes [[4]](#source-4):

| Recommendation | Strength |
| --- | --- |
| Aerobic exercise | Strong |
| Resistance (strength) training | Strong |
| Balance training | Strong |
| Gait training and task-specific practice | Strong |
| External cues (such as a beat or lines on the floor) | Strong |
| Community-based exercise | Strong |
| Behavior-change approaches to keep you exercising | Moderate |
| Flexibility exercises | Weak |

The Parkinson's Foundation recommends at least 2.5 hours of exercise a week, covering four areas: aerobic activity, strength training, balance, agility and multitasking, and flexibility [[3]](#source-3). It also cites its own research showing that people who started exercising earlier, for at least 2.5 hours a week, had a slower decline in quality of life than those who started later [[3]](#source-3).

## Most types of exercise help

The largest look at the evidence is a Cochrane review that pooled 154 trials with 7,837 people, mostly with mild to moderate Parkinson's and no major memory problems [[5]](#source-5). Compared with no exercise, most types of exercise improved motor symptoms, measured on a standard Parkinson's rating scale where a drop of 2.5 points is the smallest change people notice [[5]](#source-5):

Water-based exercise probably gave a large boost to quality of life [[5]](#source-5). Flexibility training alone had little or no effect on motor symptoms [[5]](#source-5). The most important finding may be the last one: across outcomes, the review found little evidence that one type of exercise beats another. The authors concluded that exercising matters most, and that the exact type may be secondary [[5]](#source-5).

The certainty of the evidence ranged from moderate to very low, and many trials were small [[5]](#source-5). Exercise was described as relatively safe. Falls and pain were the side effects reported most often, though few trials tracked safety well [[5]](#source-5).

## What specific trials found

### Higher-intensity aerobic exercise

In the SPARX trial, 128 people diagnosed within the past 5 years who were not yet taking Parkinson's medication were assigned to treadmill exercise 4 days a week at high intensity (80% to 85% of maximum heart rate), moderate intensity (60% to 65%), or a wait list for 6 months [[6]](#source-6). Motor scores barely changed in the high-intensity group, by 0.3 points, while they worsened by 3.2 points in the wait-list group. The moderate-intensity group did not meet the study's bar for further testing [[6]](#source-6). This was a phase 2 trial, designed to see whether a larger one was worth doing, so its results are promising rather than final [[6]](#source-6).

Exercise does not have to happen in a gym. In the Dutch Park-in-Shape trial, 130 people with mild Parkinson's who had been inactive rode a stationary bike at home for 30 to 45 minutes, 3 times a week for 6 months, with a video-game style screen, a motivational app and remote coaching [[7]](#source-7). Compared with a group that did stretching, the cycling group's motor scores, measured off medication, were 4.2 points better [[7]](#source-7).

### Tai chi for balance

In a US trial, 195 people with Parkinson's did tai chi, resistance training or stretching for 60 minutes twice a week for 24 weeks [[8]](#source-8). The tai chi group did better than both other groups on tests of how far and how steadily they could lean without losing balance. It also beat stretching on every other measure, beat resistance training on stride length and reach, and had fewer falls than the stretching group. The benefits were still there 3 months after the classes ended, and there were no serious side effects [[8]](#source-8).

### Big, exaggerated movements

LSVT BIG is a program built around practicing large, high-effort movements. In a German trial of 60 people, it improved motor scores by about 5 points at 16 weeks, while Nordic walking and unsupervised home exercise did not, and it also improved walking speed and a timed test of getting up and walking [[9]](#source-9). Quality of life did not differ between groups [[9]](#source-9). In the larger Cochrane review, the evidence for LSVT BIG was very uncertain [[5]](#source-5).

### Exercise and falls

Falls are a major concern in Parkinson's. An Australian trial assigned 231 people to a minimally supervised program targeting balance, leg strength and freezing of gait, done 40 to 60 minutes, 3 times a week for 6 months, or to usual care [[10]](#source-10). Overall, the program did not reduce falls. But people with milder disease had about 69% fewer falls, while those with more severe disease showed a trend toward more falls. After the program, the exercise group scored better than the usual-care group on physical performance, fear of falling, mood and quality of life [[10]](#source-10).

That result is one reason a tailored program matters. As Parkinson's progresses, the right balance between challenge and safety changes.

## How to start safely

The Parkinson's Foundation offers practical advice [[3]](#source-3):

- **Get evaluated first.** Start with a physical therapist who specializes in Parkinson's for a full assessment and recommendations, especially if your mobility is already affected.
- **Build gradually.** If you have been inactive, start with something low intensity like walking. A step counter can help you track your daily average and build from there.
- **Warm up and cool down** every time.
- **Know your limits,** and exercise in a way that is safe for you.
- **Find a partner or a class.** A workout partner or group leader can keep you motivated and help if needed.

Pick something you enjoy and can keep doing. The trials that showed benefits lasted months, not weeks [[6]](#source-6) [[7]](#source-7) [[8]](#source-8), and the guideline gives a strong recommendation to community-based exercise and a moderate one to approaches that help people keep exercising over the long term [[4]](#source-4).

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can turn the general advice into a plan that fits your stage and your goals. Based on the guideline, that plan may include [[4]](#source-4):

- Aerobic training at an intensity that challenges you safely
- Strength training for the legs and trunk
- Balance training and practice with turning, stepping and getting up from a chair or the floor
- Gait training, including cues such as a metronome beat or lines on the floor to help with shuffling or freezing
- Practice with the specific tasks that have become hard for you
- A bridge to community classes so you can keep going on your own

Your therapist can also adjust the program as your symptoms change, which matters given that the same falls program helped people with milder disease more than those with more severe disease [[10]](#source-10).

## The takeaway

- Parkinson's disease causes tremor, slow movement and stiff muscles, and often balance problems that raise the risk of falls.
- Most types of exercise improve motor symptoms and quality of life compared with not exercising. Doing it consistently may matter more than which type you choose.
- Dance, gait and balance training, cycling, treadmill work, strength training and tai chi have all shown benefits in trials.
- Aim for at least 2.5 hours a week across aerobic, strength, balance and flexibility work, building up gradually.
- A physical therapist who knows Parkinson's can tailor the program to your stage and help you train safely as things change.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If you or a family member has Parkinson's disease, an evaluation can check walking, balance and strength and build an exercise plan you can keep up for the long haul, alongside the care from your neurologist.
