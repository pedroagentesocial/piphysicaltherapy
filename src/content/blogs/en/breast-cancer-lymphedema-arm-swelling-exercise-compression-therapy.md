Months after breast cancer surgery, your rings feel tight on one hand. Your sleeve leaves a mark on one arm, and that arm feels heavy and full by the end of the day. You may also have been told for years to never lift anything heavy with that arm, and to avoid the gym.

Those changes can be early signs of lymphedema, one of the most common long-term effects of breast cancer treatment [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). It cannot be cured, but it can be managed, and it is easier to control when treatment starts early [[1]](#source-1). And the advice to avoid lifting has been overturned by randomized trials [[8]](#source-8) [[9]](#source-9). Here is what the evidence shows.

## What lymphedema is

The lymph system is a network of vessels and lymph nodes that carries fluid through the body and helps fight infection [[1]](#source-1). Breast cancer surgery often removes lymph nodes from the armpit, and radiation can scar the lymph vessels. When the fluid cannot drain well, it builds up between the skin and the muscle and causes swelling in the hand, arm or chest on the treated side [[1]](#source-1).

Lymphedema can appear soon after treatment or years later, and it usually develops slowly [[1]](#source-1).

The authors concluded that more than 1 in 5 women who survive breast cancer will develop arm lymphedema. It became more common over the first 2 years after surgery [[2]](#source-2). The risk factors with the strongest evidence were more extensive surgery (removing more lymph nodes, or a mastectomy) and being overweight or having obesity [[2]](#source-2). Having lymph nodes removed in a sentinel node biopsy carried about a quarter of the risk of a full axillary dissection [[2]](#source-2).

## Signs to watch for

Contact your care team if you notice [[1]](#source-1):

- A heavy, full or tight feeling in the arm, hand, fingers or chest on the treated side
- Swelling that makes one arm look bigger than the other, or a dent that stays when you press the skin
- Rings, watches or sleeves that suddenly feel tight
- Numbness, tingling, weakness or difficulty moving the arm

Call your doctor right away if the swollen area becomes red, warm and painful, or if you have a fever. These can be signs of cellulitis, a skin infection that is more common with lymphedema and can become serious [[1]](#source-1).

## Why early detection matters

Lymphedema is staged from 0 to III. In the early stages, swelling still leaves a dent and can go down with rest and raising the arm. In later stages, the tissue becomes firm and the swelling no longer goes down with rest [[1]](#source-1).

That is why some cancer programs measure arm volume before surgery and then regularly afterward. In a US study of 196 women, arm volume was measured before surgery and every 3 months after. In the 43 women who developed lymphedema, it showed up on average about 7 months after surgery [[3]](#source-3). When a small increase was found, women wore a compression sleeve for about 4 weeks. Their arm volume went down, and the reduction held about 5 months later [[3]](#source-3). Tools such as bioimpedance spectroscopy can also pick up fluid buildup before symptoms appear [[1]](#source-1).

## What the trials show

| Question | What a randomized trial found |
| --- | --- |
| Does wearing a compression sleeve after surgery prevent swelling? | In 307 women who had axillary lymph node dissection, sleeves worn until 3 months after treatment ended lowered the risk of arm swelling. At 1 year, 14% vs. 25% had swelling by arm volume measurement [[4]](#source-4) |
| Does adding manual lymph drainage after surgery prevent lymphedema? | In 160 women, adding lymph drainage massage to guidelines and exercise did not lower the rate of lymphedema at 1 year (24% vs. 19%) [[5]](#source-5) |
| Is massage plus bandaging better than a compression garment alone for existing lymphedema? | In 95 women, daily lymph drainage and bandaging reduced excess arm volume by 29.0% vs. 22.6% with a compression garment alone, a difference that was not significant [[6]](#source-6) |
| Does weight lifting worsen existing lymphedema? | In 141 women, slowly progressive weight lifting did not increase swelling and led to fewer flare-ups (14% vs. 29%) [[8]](#source-8) |
| Does weight lifting raise the risk of lymphedema? | In 154 women at risk, lymphedema developed in 11% of those who lifted and 17% of those who did not [[9]](#source-9) |

## Lifting weights is safe, and may help

For years, women were told to protect the arm by avoiding upper body exercise. The research has turned that advice around.

- **In women with lymphedema:** the PAL trial randomly assigned 141 breast cancer survivors with stable lymphedema to twice-weekly, slowly progressive weight lifting or a control group [[8]](#source-8). After a year, the share with a 5% or greater increase in swelling was the same, 11% vs. 12%. The lifting group had fewer flare-ups of lymphedema (14% vs. 29%), milder symptoms and more strength, with no serious problems from the exercise. Women in that trial wore a well-fitted compression garment while lifting [[8]](#source-8).
- **In women at risk:** a companion trial of 154 survivors with at least 2 lymph nodes removed found that lifting did not raise the risk of lymphedema. Among women with 5 or more nodes removed, 7% of lifters developed it compared with 22% of non-lifters [[9]](#source-9).
- **Earlier trial:** a 6-month weight training program in 45 survivors who had had axillary dissection did not cause or worsen lymphedema [[7]](#source-7).
- **Across studies:** a 2016 review of exercise studies found no effect of aerobic, resistance or mixed exercise on lymphedema or its symptoms. Exercise did not make it worse [[10]](#source-10).

One important detail: in these trials, the lifting was *slowly progressive*, and in the trial of women at risk it began with 13 weeks of supervised instruction [[8]](#source-8) [[9]](#source-9). Starting light and building up is part of what made it safe. The review found too few studies to say whether a compression garment is needed during exercise [[10]](#source-10).

## Treatment for lymphedema

Treatment can be done at home or with a certified lymphedema therapist, a physical or occupational therapist trained in lymphedema [[1]](#source-1). Options include [[1]](#source-1):

- **Compression:** sleeves, gloves, short-stretch bandaging or wraps to keep fluid from building up
- **Manual lymphatic drainage:** a gentle massage that moves lymph fluid, usually as part of complete decongestive therapy with bandaging, exercise and skin care
- **Compression pumps:** a sleeve connected to a device that applies pressure on and off
- **Surgery** for advanced cases, such as connecting lymph vessels to veins, transferring lymph nodes or liposuction

## Daily habits that help

The National Cancer Institute suggests [[1]](#source-1):

- **Protect your skin.** Wear gloves for gardening and cooking, use lotion to prevent cracks and call your doctor about any redness.
- **Exercise.** Exercise acts as a natural pump for the lymph system.
- **Raise the arm** above heart level when you sit or rest.
- **Keep a healthy weight**, since extra weight is a risk factor.
- **Avoid pressure** on the arm from tight jewelry, tight sleeves or heavy bags, and ask for blood pressure and blood draws on the other arm.
- **Avoid extreme heat** and protect your skin from the sun.

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist, especially one certified in lymphedema care, can:

- Measure both arms and track changes over time so swelling is caught early [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3)
- Fit and teach you to use compression sleeves, gloves or bandages [[1]](#source-1) [[4]](#source-4)
- Provide complete decongestive therapy, including manual lymph drainage, when it fits your case [[1]](#source-1)
- Build a slowly progressive strength program that is safe for the arm and lowers the risk of flare-ups [[8]](#source-8) [[9]](#source-9)
- Watch for signs of infection and refer you when needed [[1]](#source-1)

## The takeaway

- About 1 in 5 women develop arm lymphedema after breast cancer, with the highest risk after axillary lymph node dissection and with excess weight.
- Early signs include heaviness, tightness and rings or sleeves that feel tight. Lymphedema is easier to control when caught early.
- In a randomized trial, compression sleeves worn after node dissection lowered the risk of arm swelling. Adding lymph drainage massage after surgery did not prevent lymphedema.
- Slowly progressive weight lifting is safe for women with or at risk of lymphedema. It did not increase swelling, and it reduced flare-ups.
- Redness, warmth, pain or fever in the arm can mean cellulitis and needs a call to your doctor right away.
