Many headaches seem to come with a stiff, sore neck, and it is natural to wonder whether the neck is the cause. Sometimes it is. Physical therapy can help with some types of headache, but not all of them, and part of a good evaluation is making sure nothing more serious is going on.

**If you have a sudden, severe headache or a headache with other warning signs, go straight to the end of this article.**

## Three kinds of headache, three different problems

The International Headache Society classifies headaches by how they behave [[1]](#source-1):

- **Tension-type headache:** usually on both sides, with a pressing or tightening feeling, mild to moderate, and not made worse by routine activity. It lasts from 30 minutes to 7 days [[1]](#source-1), and the pain can spread into or from the neck [[7]](#source-7).
- **Cervicogenic headache:** a headache caused by a problem in the neck itself, usually but not always with neck pain [[2]](#source-2). It tends to stay on one side, can be triggered by neck movement or by pressing on neck muscles, and may spread from the back of the head toward the front, although none of these features is unique to it [[2]](#source-2).
- **Migraine:** attacks lasting 4 to 72 hours, often on one side and throbbing, moderate to severe, made worse by activity, and with nausea or sensitivity to light and sound [[1]](#source-1). Migraine needs a different treatment plan, usually led by a doctor.

One important point: changes on a scan of the upper neck are common in people who have no headaches at all, so they suggest but do not prove that the neck is the cause [[2]](#source-2).

Tension-type headache is the most common kind [[4]](#source-4). Cervicogenic headache is much harder to count: estimates range from 0.17% to 69% depending on how studies define and diagnose it [[6]](#source-6). The only population study using the international criteria found 3.9%, and the authors of that review describe it as a rare headache disorder [[5]](#source-5).

## What physical therapy can do

**For headaches that come from the neck,** the physical therapy guideline for neck pain recommends, at a moderate level of evidence [[8]](#source-8):

- **Early on:** supervised exercises to keep the neck moving, plus self-mobilization exercises for the upper neck
- **After the first weeks:** hands-on mobilization or manipulation of the neck
- **When it has lasted longer:** mobilization or manipulation combined with stretching, strengthening and endurance exercises for the neck and shoulder area

The best-known trial included 200 people with cervicogenic headache. Six weeks of either manual therapy or specific neck exercise reduced how often and how badly headaches hit, and the benefit was still there after 12 months; combining both was not significantly better than either one alone [[10]](#source-10). Even so, 25% of patients did not reach a 50% reduction in headache frequency [[11]](#source-11).

A 2026 review of 29 trials adds caution: manual therapy reduced headache intensity compared with a sham treatment in the first 2 weeks, but not at 12 months, and the evidence was low certainty [[12]](#source-12). The honest summary is that physical therapy helps many people with this type of headache, but long-term results vary.

**For tension-type headache,** the evidence points toward exercise and soft-tissue work rather than spinal manipulation:

- A guideline on headaches with neck pain suggests low-load endurance exercises for the neck and shoulders, and for chronic cases, general exercise, combined care or massage. It advises against offering neck manipulation as the only treatment [[9]](#source-9).
- A review of 15 trials found that thrust manipulation was no better than no treatment for tension-type headache, while soft-tissue techniques did help, based on low-certainty evidence [[13]](#source-13).
- Strength training reduced pain intensity in tension-type headache, but the certainty of that evidence was very low [[14]](#source-14).

**What about dry needling?** In a trial of 142 people with cervicogenic headache, people treated with manipulation and electrical dry needling were more likely to report success at 3 months than those treated with mobilization and exercise (77% vs. 15%) [[15]](#source-15). The trial had no sham group and only short follow-up, so the long-term value is still unknown.

## What physical therapy cannot do

- **It cannot rule out a dangerous cause on its own.** Guidelines say serious conditions and migraine should be ruled out first [[9]](#source-9), and a good therapist will refer you when something does not fit.
- **It does not work for everyone.** As in the trial above, a meaningful share of people do not improve enough [[11]](#source-11).
- **It has risks, although serious ones are rare.** Reported side effects of treatment are usually minor and short-lived, such as local soreness or dizziness, but rare serious events after neck manipulation are known to occur [[8]](#source-8).

## When painkillers make headaches worse

Taking pain relievers too often can cause what is called medication-overuse headache, or rebound headache [[3]](#source-3) [[16]](#source-16). It is diagnosed when someone with an existing headache problem has headaches on 15 or more days a month and has regularly overused headache medicine for more than 3 months [[3]](#source-3):

| Medicine | Overuse threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Acetaminophen, aspirin or other anti-inflammatories such as ibuprofen | 15 or more days a month |
| Triptans (migraine medicines) | 10 or more days a month |
| Opioids | 10 or more days a month |
| Combination pain relievers | 10 or more days a month |

*Source: International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition [[3]](#source-3).*

It usually, but not always, improves once the overuse stops [[3]](#source-3). As a practical rule, MedlinePlus warns that taking pain medicines more than 3 days a week may lead to rebound headaches [[18]](#source-18). Talk to your doctor before changing how you take any medicine.

## What you can do now

The NHS suggests these steps for tension headaches [[16]](#source-16):

- Exercise, yoga or massage to help you relax
- A low, firm pillow and heat or cold packs if you have neck pain with your headaches
- Better sleep habits, and cutting down on caffeine

MedlinePlus adds keeping a headache diary and practicing relaxation or stress management, and notes that holding your head in one position for a long time, as in computer work, can be a trigger [[18]](#source-18). See a doctor if your headaches come several times a week or are severe [[16]](#source-16). The NINDS notes that a doctor can recommend adding physical therapy, massage and gentle exercise to a treatment plan [[17]](#source-17).

## Warning signs: when a headache is an emergency

**Call 911 right away** if you have [[16]](#source-16) [[18]](#source-18) [[19]](#source-19):

- A sudden, extremely painful headache, or the worst headache of your life
- A headache after a head injury
- A headache with confusion, drowsiness, or problems with speech, memory, vision, movement or balance
- Numbness or weakness, especially on one side of the body
- A headache with a high fever or a stiff neck, or with repeated vomiting

Get urgent medical advice for a severe headache with jaw pain when eating, blurred or double vision, or a sore scalp [[16]](#source-16), or for a headache that keeps getting worse over days or weeks or changes its usual pattern [[17]](#source-17).

## The takeaway

- Tension-type headache is the most common kind. Headaches that truly start in the neck are less common.
- For neck-related headaches, manual therapy and exercise are guideline-recommended and help many people, though not everyone.
- For tension-type headache, exercise and soft-tissue work have more support than manipulation.
- Using pain relievers too often can make headaches worse.
- A sudden, severe headache or a headache with neurological symptoms is an emergency: call 911.

This article is not a substitute for medical care. You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If your headaches come with neck pain or stiffness, an evaluation can check whether your neck is involved, and we will refer you to a doctor if we see signs of something else.
