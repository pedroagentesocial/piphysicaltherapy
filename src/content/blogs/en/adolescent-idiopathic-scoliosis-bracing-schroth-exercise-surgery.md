Your daughter bends over to tie her shoes and you notice that one side of her back sits higher than the other. Or the school nurse sends home a note, or the pediatrician pauses during a checkup and orders an X-ray. Now you have a word, scoliosis, and many questions: Will it get worse? Did a heavy backpack cause it? Does she need a brace, special exercises or surgery?

Most teens with scoliosis have small curves that never need treatment [[1]](#source-1). For the curves that do, the treatment plan depends mostly on the size of the curve and how much growing is left [[1]](#source-1). Here is what the evidence shows.

## What adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is

Scoliosis is a sideways curve of the spine. The bones also twist, so the spine looks more like a "C" or an "S" than a straight line [[1]](#source-1). "Idiopathic" means the exact cause is unknown, and "adolescent" means it shows up around puberty [[1]](#source-1). A curve counts as scoliosis when it measures at least 10 degrees on an X-ray [[3]](#source-3).

What it is *not* caused by: carrying a heavy backpack or poor posture [[1]](#source-1). Genes play a role in some cases, and about 30% of teens with it have a family history [[1]](#source-1).

## How common it is

Studies report that 0.47% to 5.2% of adolescents have it [[2]](#source-2). Girls are affected more often, and the gap widens as curves get bigger: the ratio is about 1.4 girls to every boy for curves of 10 to 20 degrees, and about 7 to 1 for curves over 40 degrees [[2]](#source-2).

## Signs to look for

Small curves often go unnoticed until a growth spurt. Signs include [[1]](#source-1):

- Uneven shoulders, with one shoulder blade sticking out more than the other
- Ribs that stand out more on one side
- An uneven waistline
- One hip higher than the other

The standard screening test is the Adams forward bend test. Your child bends forward with feet together and arms hanging, and the doctor looks from behind for a difference in the shape of the ribs on each side [[1]](#source-1). An X-ray confirms the diagnosis and measures the curve in degrees [[1]](#source-1).

The US Preventive Services Task Force concluded in 2018 that there is not enough evidence to judge the benefits and harms of routine screening of all children ages 10 to 18 [[3]](#source-3). That is a statement about screening everyone, not about checking a child who already shows signs.

## How much it matters long term

Even without treatment, most adults with idiopathic scoliosis do well. A US study followed 117 people with untreated idiopathic scoliosis for about 50 years and compared them with people of the same age and sex without it [[4]](#source-4):

- Survival was similar to the general population.
- Chronic back pain was more common, 61% vs. 35%, but most people with pain described it as little or moderate.
- Shortness of breath during daily activities was reported by 22% vs. 15%. The risk was highest in people with very large curves (over 80 degrees) in the upper back.
- Overall, the authors found that people were productive and functional at a high level.

A curve that is above 30 degrees when growth ends does carry a higher risk of health problems and of getting worse in adulthood. That is why treatment aims to keep curves from growing large during the teen years [[7]](#source-7).

## Treatment depends on the curve and the growth left

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons describes these general ranges [[1]](#source-1):

| Curve size and growth | Usual approach |
| --- | --- |
| Under 25 degrees, or nearly done growing | Observation, with a recheck about every 6 to 12 months and follow-up X-rays until growth ends [[1]](#source-1) |
| 25 to 45 degrees and still growing | Bracing may be recommended to keep the curve from getting worse [[1]](#source-1) |
| Over 45 to 50 degrees, or progressing despite a brace | Surgery (spinal fusion) may be recommended [[1]](#source-1) |

Once a teen is fully grown, it is not common for a curve to worsen rapidly [[1]](#source-1).

## Bracing: it works, and wear time matters

A brace does not straighten a curve, but it often keeps it from worsening to the point of needing surgery [[1]](#source-1). The strongest evidence comes from BrAIST, a US trial of 242 teens with curves at high risk of getting worse [[5]](#source-5). Those who were braced were told to wear the brace at least 18 hours a day. Success meant reaching the end of growth without the curve progressing to 50 degrees or more [[5]](#source-5).

The trial was stopped early because bracing worked so well. The more hours a day teens wore the brace, the more likely they were to succeed [[5]](#source-5). A Cochrane review also found that all included studies consistently showed bracing prevented curves from progressing. Bracing did not change quality of life during treatment, though the evidence was low quality [[6]](#source-6).

Most braces are custom-made and worn under the arms. Loose clothing covers them, and teens can usually take the brace off for sports [[1]](#source-1).

## What scoliosis-specific exercises can and cannot do

Physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercises, such as the Schroth method, are designed around the three-dimensional shape of the curve and often train active self-correction [[8]](#source-8) [[9]](#source-9). Generic exercise programs are not specific to scoliosis [[7]](#source-7). Several trials have tested specific exercises:

- **Schroth in Turkey:** 45 teens were split into supervised Schroth exercises in a clinic, the same exercises at home or no treatment. After 24 weeks, only the supervised clinic group improved its curve angle, rotation, rib hump and waist asymmetry. The curves got worse in the group with no treatment [[8]](#source-8).
- **Self-correction in Italy:** 110 teens with mild curves under 25 degrees did either active self-correction and task-oriented exercises or traditional spinal exercises until the end of growth. The specific exercise group improved by more than 5 degrees, while the traditional exercise group stayed stable. The benefit lasted at least a year after treatment ended [[9]](#source-9).
- **Exercise in everyday clinics:** in an Italian study of 293 teens with curves of 11 to 20 degrees, those who did scoliosis-specific exercises were less likely to need a brace than those who had no therapy [[10]](#source-10).

The 2024 Cochrane review is more cautious. It included 13 trials with 583 teens [[7]](#source-7):

- Compared with no treatment, exercise reduced the curve by about 3.6 degrees, but the evidence was very uncertain.
- Adding scoliosis-specific exercise to a brace may reduce the curve by about 2 degrees more than a brace alone.
- In the one trial that followed teens to the end of growth, 38 in 100 who did generic exercise progressed by more than 5 degrees, compared with 7 in 100 who did scoliosis-specific exercise.
- The authors concluded that the evidence is still sparse and that more trials are needed.

The US Preventive Services Task Force also rated the evidence on exercise as inadequate [[3]](#source-3). In practice, exercise is best seen as something that may help with mild curves and can be added to a brace, not as a replacement for a brace when one is recommended [[1]](#source-1) [[7]](#source-7).

## When surgery is needed

Surgery may be recommended if a curve is over 45 to 50 degrees or if bracing has not stopped it from getting there [[1]](#source-1). The standard operation is spinal fusion. The curved vertebrae are realigned, held with metal rods and fused into a single solid bone [[1]](#source-1). Fusion is very successful at stopping the curve from growing [[1]](#source-1).

Recovery usually goes like this [[1]](#source-1):

- Most teens walk the day after surgery and go home after 3 to 4 days.
- Most return to school and daily activities within 4 weeks.
- Most return to sports within 6 to 9 months, though contact sports such as football may be discouraged.
- Spinal fusion does not raise the risk of problems in future pregnancies or childbirth.

A newer surgery that tethers the spine without fusion is being studied in younger teens with moderate curves, but longer follow-up is still needed [[1]](#source-1).

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can:

- Teach scoliosis-specific exercises for self-correction and practice them under supervision, since supervised Schroth did better than home exercises alone in one trial [[8]](#source-8) [[9]](#source-9)
- Work alongside the orthopedic team when a brace is prescribed, since exercise may add a small benefit to bracing [[7]](#source-7)
- Help teens stay active and in sports, which is usually possible with a brace [[1]](#source-1)
- Help with back pain, which is more common in adults with scoliosis [[4]](#source-4)
- Guide the return to activity after spinal fusion [[1]](#source-1)

## The takeaway

- Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is common, it is not caused by backpacks or posture and most curves stay small.
- Treatment depends on curve size and how much growing is left: observation for small curves, bracing for moderate curves in a growing teen and surgery for large curves.
- Bracing works. In the BrAIST trial, 72% of braced teens avoided progression to 50 degrees, compared with 48% of those observed, and more hours in the brace meant better results.
- Scoliosis-specific exercises such as Schroth may help mild curves and add a little to bracing, but the evidence is still limited.
- Spinal fusion reliably stops a large curve, and most teens are back in school within a month.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If your teen has been diagnosed with scoliosis, an evaluation can review the plan from the orthopedic team and teach scoliosis-specific exercises that fit alongside observation or a brace.
