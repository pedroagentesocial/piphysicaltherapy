You wake up and your ring and little fingers are numb. It happens again while you drive with your elbow bent on the door, or after a long phone call. Maybe you have started dropping things, or your hand feels clumsy when you pinch.

Those symptoms often point to cubital tunnel syndrome, a squeezed nerve at the elbow. It is the second most common nerve entrapment in the arm, after carpal tunnel syndrome [[3]](#source-3) [[8]](#source-8). Most mild cases improve without surgery once you know what irritates the nerve and change a few habits [[1]](#source-1) [[6]](#source-6). Here is what the evidence shows.

## What cubital tunnel syndrome is

The ulnar nerve runs through a tunnel behind the bony bump on the inner side of the elbow. That is the spot people call the "funny bone," where a knock sends a shock down the arm [[1]](#source-1). The nerve gives feeling to the little finger and half of the ring finger and controls many of the small muscles of the hand [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2).

Bending the elbow stretches the nerve over the bone and can reduce its blood supply [[1]](#source-1). That is why symptoms often show up with the elbow bent for a long time. Common triggers include [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2):

- Leaning on the inner part of the elbow, for example on a desk, an armrest or a car window
- Keeping the elbow bent for long periods, such as while holding a phone or driving
- Sleeping with the arm folded and the elbow bent

Things that raise the risk include a previous elbow fracture or dislocation, arthritis or bone spurs, diabetes and repetitive bending. It usually shows up in middle age [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2).

## How common it is

In an Italian province of about 250,000 people, about 25 of every 100,000 people were diagnosed each year, with men affected almost twice as often as women. The average age was 56, and rates were higher in an area dominated by manual work [[3]](#source-3). In UK general practices, new cases were most common between ages 55 and 64, and about 30% of people who saw their doctor for it went on to have an operation [[4]](#source-4).

## Symptoms

Common symptoms include [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2):

- Numbness and tingling in the ring and little fingers
- Pain on the inner side of the elbow
- Symptoms that are worse at night or first thing in the morning, or when the elbow is bent or leaned on
- Weakness when pinching, a weaker grip, clumsiness or a tendency to drop things

In severe cases, feeling in the ring and little fingers can be lost [[2]](#source-2).

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if your symptoms are severe, or if milder symptoms last more than 6 weeks [[1]](#source-1). This matters because a nerve that stays compressed can lead to wasting of the hand muscles, and that wasting cannot be reversed [[1]](#source-1).

The diagnosis is based mainly on your symptoms and an exam. Your doctor may tap over the nerve to see if it triggers tingling, test feeling and strength in the hand and check your neck to rule out a pinched nerve there [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). Nerve conduction studies can confirm the diagnosis and show how badly the nerve is compressed, and X-rays can look for bone spurs or arthritis [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2).

## Most mild cases improve without surgery

Several studies suggest that understanding the problem and avoiding the positions that squeeze the nerve is the foundation of treatment:

In an Italian study, 24 people with nerve-test-confirmed cubital tunnel syndrome were given an explanation of the problem and how to avoid positions that worsen it, but no other treatment. A year later, about half reported improvement, and their nerve tests had improved too. Most said they had changed how they held their arm [[5]](#source-5).

A Swedish trial took 70 people with mild or moderate symptoms, explained the cause and how to avoid provoking it, and then assigned them to a night splint, nerve gliding exercises or no extra treatment [[6]](#source-6). At 6 months, 89.5% had improved, with no difference between the groups. Splints and nerve glides did not add to the benefit of the advice [[6]](#source-6).

A US study of 19 people treated with activity changes plus a rigid splint worn at night for 3 months found that 88% of treated arms avoided surgery about 2 years later. Arm function scores, grip strength and quality of life all improved [[7]](#source-7).

The 2025 Cochrane review agrees that in mild cases, information on movements and positions to avoid may reduce discomfort, though that conclusion rests on a small trial [[8]](#source-8).

## What you can do day to day

Practical steps that ease pressure on the nerve [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2):

- **Do not lean on your elbow.** Avoid hard surfaces under the inner elbow, or wear an elbow pad over the "funny bone."
- **Keep the elbow straighter.** Set your chair high enough that you do not rest your elbow on the armrest, and leave more space between your desk and your chest.
- **Change how you drive.** Do not rest your arm on an open window.
- **Keep the elbow straight at night.** A padded brace or a towel wrapped around the elbow can keep it from bending while you sleep.
- **Try nerve gliding exercises** if your therapist recommends them. The NHS Scotland sheet suggests lifting your hands above your head, placing them over your ears and lowering them to your sides, 10 times in a row, twice a day.
- **Stay at work if you can.** You do not need to be free of symptoms to work [[2]](#source-2).

Anti-inflammatory medicines such as ibuprofen may help with swelling. Steroid injections are generally avoided because of the risk of injuring the nerve [[1]](#source-1), and the Cochrane review found it unclear whether they help [[8]](#source-8).

## When surgery makes sense

Surgery is considered when nonsurgical treatment has not helped, the nerve is badly compressed, or the compression has caused weakness or muscle damage [[1]](#source-1). The two main approaches are [[1]](#source-1):

- **Cubital tunnel release (simple decompression):** the roof of the tunnel is divided to make more room for the nerve.
- **Ulnar nerve transposition:** the nerve is moved to the front of the bony bump so it no longer stretches over it when the elbow bends.

How do they compare? Trials have found little difference in results:

| Study | What it found |
| --- | --- |
| Dutch trial, 152 people (2005) | Good or excellent results in 65% with simple decompression and 70% with transposition, not a significant difference. Complications: 9.6% vs. 31.1% [[9]](#source-9) |
| Meta-analysis, 4 trials (2007) | No difference in symptom scores or nerve conduction between the two [[10]](#source-10) |
| Cochrane review, 15 trials, 970 people (2025) | Little to no difference in function or complications between simple decompression and transposition, even when the nerve damage is severe. Endoscopic and open release also had similar results [[8]](#source-8) |

Because simple decompression is the simpler operation with fewer complications, the authors of the Dutch trial recommended it [[9]](#source-9). Your surgeon will choose based on where and how the nerve is compressed, including whether it slips out of place when the elbow bends [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2).

### Recovery after surgery

Both operations are usually done as outpatient procedures. A splint is often worn for a few days to several weeks, or 3 to 6 weeks after a deeper transposition beneath the muscle [[1]](#source-1). Gentle elbow movement often starts early to limit scarring around the nerve, and heavy lifting is avoided for a few weeks [[1]](#source-1). Physical therapy can help restore strength and motion. Nerves recover slowly, so it may take a long time to know the final result, and people who already have severe compression or muscle wasting may not recover fully [[1]](#source-1).

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can:

- Look at how you work, drive, use your phone and sleep, and find the positions that bend or press on your elbow [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)
- Help set up your workstation so your elbows stay straighter [[2]](#source-2)
- Fit or advise on a night splint or padded brace [[1]](#source-1) [[7]](#source-7)
- Teach nerve gliding exercises if they are a good fit [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)
- Track grip and pinch strength and flag signs that the nerve is getting worse, so you are referred before muscle wasting sets in [[1]](#source-1)
- Guide rehab after surgery to restore motion and strength [[1]](#source-1)

## The takeaway

- Numbness and tingling in the ring and little fingers, worse with the elbow bent, is often cubital tunnel syndrome.
- Most mild to moderate cases improve with advice and changes in how you use your arm. Night splints may help some people.
- See a doctor if symptoms are severe or last more than 6 weeks, and promptly if your hand is getting weak, because muscle wasting cannot be reversed.
- When surgery is needed, simple decompression and moving the nerve give similar results, and simple decompression has fewer complications.
- Nerves recover slowly, so patience is part of recovery.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If your ring and little fingers keep going numb, an evaluation can find the habits that are squeezing the nerve, check your hand strength and build a plan to calm the nerve down.
