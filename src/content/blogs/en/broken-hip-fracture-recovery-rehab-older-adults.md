A slip on an icy driveway, a trip over a rug at night, a missed step. For an older adult, a fall like this can end in a broken hip, and life can change in an instant: an ambulance, surgery, days in the hospital and then the long work of learning to walk again.

A hip fracture is serious, but recovery is possible, and physical therapy plays a central part. Here is what to expect and what the research says helps.

## How common it is

In the United States, nearly 319,000 adults 65 and older are hospitalized for a hip fracture every year, and falls cause 88% of emergency visits and hospital stays for hip fractures in this age group [[1]](#source-1). Most of these breaks happen in bones weakened by osteoporosis, where even a simple trip or twist can be enough [[2]](#source-2).

The signs are usually clear: pain in the groin or upper thigh, not being able to stand or put weight on the leg, and a leg that looks shorter or turned outward [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3). Some breaks that have not shifted out of place hurt less at first, and a person may still be able to walk on them [[2]](#source-2).

## Surgery comes first

Almost every hip fracture needs surgery [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3). Depending on the type of break and the person's age, the surgeon either fixes the bone with screws, a plate, a rod or a nail, or replaces part or all of the joint [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3). The operation usually takes place the same day or the day after arriving at the hospital [[3]](#source-3). Having surgery within 24 to 48 hours may lower the risk of complications, although getting medically ready for surgery still comes first [[2]](#source-2).

The main reason for surgery is to relieve pain and get the person out of bed. Long bed rest brings its own dangers: bed sores, blood clots, pneumonia and, in very old patients, confusion that makes rehabilitation much harder [[2]](#source-2).

## Getting up early matters

Most patients get out of bed and start physical therapy the day after surgery [[2]](#source-2). If you had a hip replacement, you can usually put full weight on the leg right away. If the bone was fixed with metal, your surgeon will tell you how much weight it can take [[2]](#source-2).

Large studies show why the first day counts. Among 126,897 people aged 60 and older who had hip fracture surgery in England and Wales, the 79% who got up on the day of surgery or the day after were more likely to be alive and walking 30 days later [[6]](#source-6):

| At 30 days | Up early (day of or day after surgery) | Up later (2 or more days after) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alive | 95.9% | 92.4% |
| Able to walk indoors again | 81.2% | 73.8% |
| Able to walk outdoors again | 9.7% | 7.9% |

A study of 701 patients found the same pattern: the more a person could do on the first day after surgery, such as getting out of bed, standing up from a chair and walking indoors, the lower the risk of dying within 30 days [[7]](#source-7). These studies observe patients rather than assign them at random, and people who are sicker are often less able to get up early, so part of the difference may come from that. Even so, both point in the same direction.

## What recovery really looks like

Recovery is measured in months, not weeks. A hip fracture usually takes several weeks or months to heal, and the hospital stay is often around 1 to 4 weeks [[3]](#source-3). Some people go home with in-home physical therapy, and others spend time in a rehabilitation facility until they can walk and manage daily activities on their own [[2]](#source-2).

It is important to be realistic. A review of 38 studies found that most of the recovery in walking and daily activities happens within the first 6 months [[5]](#source-5). Between 40% and 60% of people got back to the level of mobility they had before the fracture, and in Western countries, 10% to 20% moved into long-term care [[5]](#source-5). Some people never regain their previous strength and need a cane or walker [[3]](#source-3).

Those numbers are not a forecast for any one person. People who lived independently before the fracture tend to do better than those who already lived in residential care [[5]](#source-5), and the rehab you do makes a difference.

## What the exercise trials show

A Cochrane review gathered 40 trials with 4,059 people, average age 80, testing different ways to improve mobility after hip fracture surgery [[8]](#source-8):

- **In the hospital**, programs aimed at improving mobility may lead to a moderate, meaningful gain. Training that focused on walking, balance and everyday movements probably helped [[8]](#source-8).
- **After leaving the hospital**, exercise programs led to small but meaningful gains in mobility and walking speed, and the evidence here was rated high-certainty [[8]](#source-8).
- **Programs that combined several types of training** probably led to the largest gains [[8]](#source-8).

The help does not have to stop when formal rehab ends. In a US trial, 232 older adults who had already finished standard rehab after a hip fracture were given a home program taught by a physical therapist, with exercises such as standing up from a chair and stepping onto a step, done on their own for 6 months. Their physical function improved more than in a comparison group, and the gains were still present at 9 months. The improvement was modest, and the authors said its clinical importance still needs to be confirmed [[9]](#source-9).

At home, a few daily habits help [[4]](#source-4):

- Do the exercises your physical therapist taught you, and ask how much weight you can put on the leg.
- Try not to sit for more than 45 minutes without getting up and moving.
- Choose firm chairs with arm rests, and avoid low, soft sofas that put your knees higher than your hips.
- Remove loose rugs and cords, use good lighting and add grab bars in the bathroom to prevent another fall.

## Preventing the next fracture

One broken hip is a warning sign for the bones. After a hip fracture, ask to be checked for osteoporosis once you have healed, since there are treatments that can strengthen weak bone [[4]](#source-4).

Treatment can make a real difference. In a trial of 2,127 people who received a yearly osteoporosis infusion (zoledronic acid) or a placebo, starting within 90 days of hip fracture surgery, the treatment lowered both new fractures and deaths over a median follow-up of about 2 years [[10]](#source-10):

All participants also received vitamin D and calcium [[10]](#source-10). Whether this or another treatment fits you is a decision to make with your doctor.

Exercise and fall prevention are the other half of the plan. The strength and balance you rebuild in rehab also help keep you on your feet.

## When to get medical help

**Call 911** if an older adult has severe hip pain after a fall, cannot walk or put weight on the leg, or has tingling or loss of feeling in the hip or leg after an injury [[3]](#source-3).

**After surgery, contact the surgeon** if you have shortness of breath or chest pain when breathing, a swollen, red or warm leg, calf pain, more redness, pain or drainage at the incision, a fever above 101°F (38.3°C), or pain your medicines do not control [[4]](#source-4).

## The takeaway

- Most hip fractures in older adults come from falls and need surgery within a day or two.
- Getting up and moving the day after surgery is linked with better survival and walking a month later.
- Recovery takes months. Most of the progress comes in the first 6 months, and about half of people regain their previous mobility.
- Exercise focused on walking, balance and everyday movements helps, both in the hospital and after going home.
- Ask about an osteoporosis check and treatment. In one trial it lowered new fractures and deaths.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. After a hip fracture, once your surgeon clears you, an evaluation can measure your strength and balance and build a plan to help you walk with more confidence and lower your risk of another fall.
