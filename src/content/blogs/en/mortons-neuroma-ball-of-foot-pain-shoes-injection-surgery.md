It starts as a strange feeling in the front of your foot, as if a pebble or a folded sock were stuck under the ball of the foot. Then it turns into a burning or shooting pain that runs into two of your toes. Taking off your shoe and rubbing the foot helps. Putting on tight or high-heeled shoes makes it worse.

That pattern is typical of Morton's neuroma, one of the most common causes of pain in the ball of the foot. Most people never need surgery, but the research on the best treatment is thinner than you might expect [[1]](#source-1) [[7]](#source-7). Here is what it is, how it is diagnosed and what the studies show.

## What it is

Between the long bones of the front of the foot (the metatarsals) run small nerves that carry sensation to the toes. In Morton's neuroma, one of these nerves becomes thickened and painful where it passes between the metatarsal heads. It happens most often in the space between the third and fourth toes, and less often between the second and third [[1]](#source-1).

Despite the name, it is not a true tumor of the nerve. It is a thickening of the nerve and the tissue around it, which is why some surgeons prefer the name Morton's metatarsalgia [[2]](#source-2). The exact cause is still debated. Repeated pressure on the nerve, reduced blood supply, an inflamed bursa next to the nerve and the nerve being pinched under the ligament that links the metatarsal heads have all been proposed [[2]](#source-2).

Typical symptoms include [[1]](#source-1):

- Burning or shooting pain in the ball of the foot that spreads into the toes
- The feeling of walking on a pebble or a bunched-up sock
- Numbness or tingling in the two toes next to the painful space
- Pain that gets worse with walking, standing and tight shoes, and eases when the shoe comes off

It is more common in women. In one large trial, 85% of the 131 participants were women, and their average age was 53 [[6]](#source-6).

## How it is diagnosed

A careful history and a hands-on exam are usually enough to make the diagnosis. When the picture is unclear, ultrasound done by an experienced examiner is the imaging test of choice [[2]](#source-2).

A study of 40 patients (54 feet) compared seven clinical tests with ultrasound. Ultrasound confirmed the neuroma in the spot found on the exam in 53 of 54 feet. The most sensitive test was simply squeezing the painful space between the thumb and index finger. The well-known Mulder's click, where the examiner squeezes the front of the foot and feels a click, missed many more cases [[3]](#source-3).

The exam also matters because other problems can cause pain in the same area, such as irritation of the joints at the base of the toes, a stress fracture of a metatarsal or pain coming from a nerve higher up the leg. Sorting those out is part of a good evaluation.

## What helps

### Shoes, padding and a staged plan

Treatment usually starts with the simplest steps: shoes with a wider toe box and a lower heel, and a pad or insert that takes pressure off the metatarsal heads [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4).

In a study of 115 patients treated with a staged plan, the first step was education, footwear changes and offloading the metatarsal heads. Those who did not improve moved on to an injection, and only then to surgery. Overall, 85% felt they had improved, and 21% eventually needed surgery [[4]](#source-4). That study had no comparison group, but it shows that most people in a step-by-step plan avoided an operation.

In a randomized study of 82 patients, one group started with footwear changes and orthoses and the other with steroid injections. Satisfaction was better in the injection group at 1, 6 and 12 months. At 12 months, 82% of the injection group had complete or partial relief, compared with 63% of the footwear group, but the difference in relief was not statistically significant at one year [[5]](#source-5).

### Steroid injections

Corticosteroid injections are one of the most studied treatments [[7]](#source-7) [[8]](#source-8). In a patient-blinded trial of 131 people in Edinburgh, a steroid and numbing injection improved overall foot health more than a numbing injection alone at one and three months [[6]](#source-6). A review that pooled studies of steroid injections reported about 50% success at 12 months [[8]](#source-8).

The 2024 Cochrane review was more cautious. Pooling two trials, it found that adding a steroid to the numbing injection may make little or no difference in pain or function at three to six months, though the certainty of that evidence was low. It found moderate-certainty evidence that injections guided by ultrasound probably reduce pain and improve function more than injections given without ultrasound [[1]](#source-1). Side effects reported with steroid injections included thinning of the skin, lighter skin color and thinning of the fat pad under the foot, each in about 3% to 4% of people [[1]](#source-1).

### Manual therapy and other options

A 2019 review of 25 studies found that steroid injections and manipulation or mobilization of the foot were the two treatments with the strongest evidence for reducing pain. The evidence for mobilization came from a single trial, and the authors did not find high-quality evidence for any one "gold standard" treatment [[7]](#source-7).

Other options, such as alcohol (sclerosing) injections, radiofrequency ablation, cryoablation, shockwave therapy and Botox, have been studied mostly in small case series and still need better trials [[7]](#source-7) [[8]](#source-8).

### Surgery

When nonsurgical care does not work, surgeons can remove the thickened nerve (neurectomy) [[2]](#source-2). In a study of 78 patients followed for an average of 4.6 years, 82% reported excellent or good results, 10% fair results and 8% no improvement. Even so, 71% still had some limits on the shoes they could wear [[9]](#source-9). Because the nerve is removed, the area between the two toes is usually left numb, and success rates rarely go above about 83%, so patients should talk through expectations before surgery [[2]](#source-2).

## What recovery usually looks like

Every foot is different, but a plan built on this evidence usually:

- **Takes pressure off the nerve.** Wider, lower shoes and a pad or insert placed behind the painful metatarsal heads [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4).
- **Changes the load for a while.** Cutting back on long standing, running, jumping or activities that push the weight onto the ball of the foot, without stopping all activity.
- **Adds hands-on care and exercise.** Mobilization of the foot has some early support [[7]](#source-7), and keeping the foot and calf strong helps people get back to walking and sport.
- **Moves to an injection if needed.** Usually under ultrasound guidance, after a talk with your doctor about benefits and side effects [[1]](#source-1) [[6]](#source-6).
- **Considers surgery only after other steps.** In a staged plan, about 1 in 5 patients ended up needing it [[4]](#source-4).

Pain with swelling and tenderness over a bone, pain after a sudden increase in running or walking, or numbness that spreads beyond the toes are reasons to get checked for other problems, such as a stress fracture or a nerve problem higher up.

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can:

- Examine the foot, including the squeeze test, and look for other causes of pain in the ball of the foot [[3]](#source-3)
- Review your shoes and help you choose footwear and padding that take pressure off the nerve [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4)
- Use hands-on mobilization of the foot and build a home program for the foot, ankle and calf [[7]](#source-7)
- Adjust your walking, running or work routine so the front of the foot is not overloaded
- Plan a gradual return to the activities you enjoy
- Coordinate with your doctor or a foot and ankle specialist if an injection or surgery makes sense [[1]](#source-1) [[9]](#source-9)

## The takeaway

- Morton's neuroma is a painful thickening of a small nerve between the metatarsals, most often between the third and fourth toes.
- It causes burning pain in the ball of the foot and the feeling of walking on a pebble, and it is more common in women.
- A careful exam usually makes the diagnosis. Ultrasound helps when the picture is unclear.
- Most people improve with footwear changes, padding and, when needed, an injection. Ultrasound-guided injections appear to work better than injections given without it.
- Surgery helps most people who need it, but not everyone, and it usually leaves the toes numb.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If burning pain in the ball of your foot is making it hard to walk, work or wear your shoes, an evaluation can confirm what is going on and build a plan to take the pressure off.
