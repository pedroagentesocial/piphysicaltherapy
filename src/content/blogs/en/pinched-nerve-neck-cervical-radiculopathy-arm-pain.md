A pinched nerve in the neck, which clinicians call cervical radiculopathy, happens when a nerve root is compressed or irritated where it leaves the spine. The pain does not stay in the neck. It usually travels down the arm as a burning or sharp pain, and it can come with tingling, numbness or weakness in the shoulder, arm or hand [[1]](#source-1). Tilting the head back, turning it or straining can make it worse, and some people feel better resting a hand on top of their head [[1]](#source-1).

It can be frightening to have an arm that tingles or feels weak, but the outlook is good: most people get better without surgery.

## Who gets it and why

In a population study from Rochester, Minnesota, that followed every case over 15 years, cervical radiculopathy affected about 83 people per 100,000 each year, with a peak between ages 50 and 54 [[2]](#source-2). The C7 nerve root was the one most often involved, followed by C6 [[2]](#source-2).

In middle-aged and older adults, the cause is usually age-related wear: the discs lose height and bone spurs narrow the openings the nerves pass through. In younger people, it is more often a herniated disc, sometimes after lifting, pulling, bending or twisting [[1]](#source-1). Only about 15% of people in the Rochester study recalled physical exertion or an injury right before their symptoms began [[2]](#source-2).

## An MRI finding is not the whole story

Changes on a neck MRI are common in people who have no symptoms at all. In a study of 63 volunteers with no history of neck problems, MRI showed a herniated disc in 10% of those under 40, and narrowing of the nerve openings in 20% of those over 40. Almost 60% of the people over 40 had at least one worn or narrowed disc [[3]](#source-3). The authors warned against making surgery decisions from scans that do not match a person’s symptoms and exam [[3]](#source-3).

That does not mean imaging is never useful. It means the scan has to fit what is happening in your arm.

## How long does it last?

| Question | What studies found |
| --- | --- |
| When does it ease? | The biggest improvement tends to come in the first 4 to 6 months [[4]](#source-4) |
| Do people recover fully? | About 83% recovered completely, though that took 24 to 36 months [[4]](#source-4) |
| How do people do long term? | 90% had no symptoms or only mild limits at follow-up, a median of about 5 years later [[2]](#source-2) |
| Does it come back? | About 32% had another episode during follow-up [[2]](#source-2) |
| How many need surgery? | 26% eventually had surgery [[2]](#source-2) |

People with a workers’ compensation claim tended to recover more slowly [[4]](#source-4). Repeat episodes are common, but they usually settle without specific treatment [[1]](#source-1).

## What helps in the first weeks

A Dutch trial randomized 205 people whose symptoms had started less than a month earlier to one of three approaches [[5]](#source-5):

- A semi-hard collar and rest for 3 to 6 weeks
- 6 weeks of physical therapy, with 12 sessions plus home exercises
- Carrying on with daily activities as much as possible, with no specific treatment

Arm pain eased in all three groups. Both the collar and physical therapy reduced arm pain faster than waiting, by about 12 extra points on a 100-point scale after 6 weeks, and both reduced neck pain, which barely changed in the wait-and-see group during those first weeks [[5]](#source-5).

A collar is meant for a short period only, because wearing one for a long time can weaken the neck muscles [[1]](#source-1). A review of 11 trials found that no single conservative treatment was clearly better than the others, and that traction worked no better than sham traction. The evidence was of low quality, and people tended to improve over time whatever treatment they had [[6]](#source-6).

Physical therapy for a pinched nerve usually includes exercises to ease pain, restore neck motion and build strength, and sometimes traction [[1]](#source-1). Your doctor may also talk with you about anti-inflammatory medicine, a short course of oral steroids or a steroid injection. Injections can reduce swelling and pain, but they do not relieve the pressure from a herniated disc or a narrowed opening [[1]](#source-1).

## Surgery or physical therapy?

Surgery is usually considered when symptoms persist after a period of nonsurgical care [[1]](#source-1). A Swedish trial of 63 people compared surgery (a fusion that removes the pressure on the nerve) followed by physical therapy with the same physical therapy program alone [[7]](#source-7):

Surgery brought faster improvement in the first year and more relief of neck pain. Arm pain and disability, though, did not differ significantly between the groups, and by 2 years the difference in how people rated their symptoms was no longer significant [[7]](#source-7). Both groups improved a lot from where they started. The authors concluded that structured physical therapy should be tried before choosing surgery [[7]](#source-7).

In the Rochester study, the people who went on to surgery were more likely to have radiating pain together with loss of feeling, or measurable muscle weakness [[2]](#source-2).

## After a car accident

Most pinched nerves develop without any injury [[2]](#source-2), but a crash can strain the neck. If you have numbness, tingling or weakness in your arm or hand after an accident, a fall or a blow, contact a doctor. If you cannot move your arm or hand, have someone call 911 [[8]](#source-8). Once a serious injury has been ruled out, the same evidence on recovery applies.

## When to get medical help

**Call 911** if neck or arm pain comes with signs of a heart attack, such as shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, vomiting or jaw pain, or if you have a fever and headache with a neck so stiff you cannot touch your chin to your chest [[8]](#source-8). Arm pain does not always come from the neck: shoulder problems, heart disease and nerve entrapment at the elbow or wrist can cause similar symptoms [[1]](#source-1).

**See a doctor promptly** if you have [[8]](#source-8):

- Numbness, tingling or weakness in your arm or hand
- Trouble walking or with your balance, or loss of bladder or bowel control
- Pain that gets worse lying down, wakes you at night or is so severe you cannot get comfortable
- Symptoms that do not improve after a week of self-care

Clumsy hands, such as new trouble with handwriting or buttons, and problems with balance or walking can be signs that the spinal cord itself is being squeezed (myelopathy). That is a different condition from a pinched nerve, and it is generally treated with surgery [[9]](#source-9).

## The takeaway

- A pinched nerve in the neck causes pain, tingling or weakness down the arm, usually from age-related changes or a herniated disc.
- The biggest improvement usually comes within 4 to 6 months, and most people recover without surgery.
- MRI changes are common in people without symptoms, so a scan has to match your exam.
- A short period with a collar, or physical therapy, eased arm pain faster than waiting it out.
- Surgery can speed up early relief for some people, but the difference shrinks by 2 years. Clumsy hands, trouble walking or worsening weakness need prompt care.
