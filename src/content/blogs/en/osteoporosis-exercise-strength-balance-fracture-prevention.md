Maybe it came up after a bone density scan. Maybe it was a wrist that broke in a fall that should not have broken anything. However you found out, a diagnosis of osteoporosis often comes with a new worry: is it still safe to exercise, and will it do any good?

The short answer is yes on both counts, with some caveats. Exercise will not reverse osteoporosis, and it does not replace medicine when medicine is needed. But the right kind of training can slow bone loss, build the strength and balance that prevent falls, and, according to pooled trials, lower the number of fractures. Here is what the research shows.

## What osteoporosis is

Osteoporosis is a disease in which bones lose density and mass, or their structure changes, so they become weaker and break more easily [[1]](#source-1). It is called a "silent" disease because it usually causes no symptoms until a bone breaks [[1]](#source-1). Breaks can happen from a minor fall, or even from everyday stresses like bending, lifting or coughing. Fractures in the spine can cause severe back pain, a loss of height or a stooped posture [[1]](#source-1).

It is common. In US national data from 2017–2018, 12.6% of adults aged 50 and older had osteoporosis: 19.6% of women and 4.4% of men. Another 43.1% had low bone mass, a milder loss of bone sometimes called osteopenia [[2]](#source-2).

Risk goes up with age and is higher in women, especially around and after menopause. Men are at risk too, particularly after 70. Other risk factors include a small body frame, a family history of osteoporosis or hip fracture, low levels of estrogen or testosterone, a diet low in calcium and vitamin D, long-term use of some medicines such as glucocorticoids, low physical activity, heavy drinking and smoking [[1]](#source-1).

## Getting checked

The usual test is a DXA scan, a quick, painless type of X-ray that measures bone density at the hip and spine [[4]](#source-4). The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening for all women 65 and older, and for younger postmenopausal women whose risk of fracture is higher. For men, it found the evidence too limited to make a recommendation either way [[3]](#source-3).

If your bones are thin, treatment usually combines nutrition with enough calcium, vitamin D and protein, not smoking, limiting alcohol, fall prevention and, for many people, medicine to slow bone loss or build bone [[4]](#source-4). Exercise is one part of that plan.

## What exercise can and cannot do

| Goal | What the evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| Bone density | Small but possibly important gains compared with no exercise, based on low-quality trials [[5]](#source-5) |
| Fractures | In a pooled analysis of 20 trials, exercise lowered low-trauma fractures by about a third, with larger effects when the training was supervised [[6]](#source-6) |
| Falls | Balance and functional exercises reduce falls in older adults by about a quarter, based on high-certainty evidence [[7]](#source-7) |
| Strength and function | A supervised heavy-lifting program improved strength and everyday function in women with low bone mass [[8]](#source-8) |

### Bone density: modest gains

A Cochrane review of 43 trials with 4,320 postmenopausal women found that exercise had a relatively small but statistically significant effect on bone density compared with no exercise [[5]](#source-5). For the hip, the most effective approach was progressive strength training for the legs. For the spine, it was programs that combined different types of exercise [[5]](#source-5). The review could not show fewer fractures, but there were too few fractures in those trials to tell, and the quality of the studies was low [[5]](#source-5).

The NIH notes another limit: in older adults, exercise no longer builds much bone mass. Its value lies in building muscle, strength, coordination and balance, which help you stay independent and avoid falls [[4]](#source-4).

### Fractures: the number that matters most

Bone density is a stand-in. What really matters is whether bones break. A 2022 analysis pooled 20 exercise trials in adults aged 45 and older and counted fractures from minor trauma, such as a fall from standing height. People who exercised had about a third fewer of these fractures, and about 31% fewer major osteoporotic fractures of the hip, spine, forearm or upper arm [[6]](#source-6).

These are pooled results from trials of varying size and design, so the exact numbers are not precise. But the direction is clear, and the authors conclude that supervision is a crucial part of exercise programs meant to prevent fractures [[6]](#source-6).

### Falls: where much of the benefit comes from

Many of these fractures start with a fall. A Cochrane review of 59 trials with almost 13,000 adults aged 60 and older found that exercise reduced the rate of falls by 23% [[7]](#source-7). Balance and functional exercises, such as standing on one leg, stepping and getting up from a chair, cut falls by 24%. Programs that combined several types, usually balance plus strength training, probably cut them by 34%. Tai chi may cut them by 19% [[7]](#source-7). The effect was larger when a health professional delivered the program [[7]](#source-7).

## Is heavy lifting safe with osteoporosis?

For years, people with low bone mass were told to stick to light exercise, out of fear that heavy loads would break a bone. The NIH still advises avoiding high-impact exercise and anything that puts sudden or excessive strain on the bones [[4]](#source-4).

An Australian trial called LIFTMOR tested that assumption carefully. It assigned 101 women, average age 65, with low bone mass to either 8 months of supervised, twice-weekly, 30-minute sessions of heavy lifting and jumping or a light home program [[8]](#source-8). In the training group, bone density rose by 2.9% at the spine, while it fell by 1.2% in the home group. At the hip, it rose by 0.3% versus a 1.9% drop. Tests of strength and everyday function also improved more with training. The only side effect reported was one mild low back spasm [[8]](#source-8).

Those results are encouraging, but the details matter. The women were screened for other health problems, the training was closely supervised with careful technique, and loads were built up over time [[8]](#source-8). This is not a reason to start heavy deadlifts on your own after a diagnosis.

## How to train safely

An international expert panel called Too Fit To Fracture put together practical advice for people with osteoporosis [[9]](#source-9):

- **Without a spine fracture**, the usual physical activity guidelines for adults are appropriate.
- **After a spine fracture**, moderate activity is preferred to vigorous activity.
- **For everyone**, daily balance training and endurance exercises for the back muscles that hold you upright are recommended.
- **Learn spine-friendly ways to move**, such as bending from the hips instead of rounding your back, rather than following blanket rules like "never lift more than 10 pounds."
- **After a spine fracture**, especially with pain, several fractures or a rounded upper back, the risks of some activities may outweigh the benefits, and a consultation with a physical therapist is recommended.

A physical therapist can test your strength and balance, show you how to lift, bend and twist in ways that protect your spine, and build a program you can progress safely [[4]](#source-4).

Your home matters too. The NIH suggests keeping floors free of clutter, securing rugs, adding grab bars in the bathroom, keeping stairs well lit with rails on both sides, avoiding walking in socks or slippers and wearing supportive shoes with nonslip soles [[4]](#source-4).

## When to see a clinician

**Get checked soon** if you have sudden back pain after a minor strain or fall, or notice that you are losing height or developing a stoop, since these can be signs of a spine fracture [[1]](#source-1).

**Ask about a bone density test** if you are a woman 65 or older, or a younger postmenopausal woman with risk factors, or if you have already broken a bone in a minor fall [[3]](#source-3).

## The takeaway

- Osteoporosis is common and often silent until a bone breaks. About 1 in 5 women 50 and older has it.
- Exercise is not a cure and does not replace medicine, but it can modestly protect bone density.
- In pooled trials, exercise lowered fractures from minor trauma by about a third, and supervised programs did better than unsupervised ones.
- Balance and strength exercises reduce falls, which lead to many fractures.
- Supervised, progressive strength training was safe and effective in one carefully run trial. Build up gradually, learn spine-friendly movement and get guidance, especially after a spine fracture.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If you have osteoporosis or low bone mass, an evaluation can check your strength and balance and build a safe, progressive plan to help protect your bones and lower your risk of a fall.
