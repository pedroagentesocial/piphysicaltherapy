The distal radius is the end of the larger forearm bone, right at the wrist, and it is where most broken wrists happen. In the US, about 1 in every 6 fractures treated in the emergency room is a distal radius fracture [[1]](#source-1).

The most common cause is falling onto an outstretched hand. In older adults with weaker bones, a minor fall can be enough. In younger people, it usually takes more force, such as a car crash or a fall from a bike or scooter [[1]](#source-1).

## Right after the injury

A broken wrist usually hurts right away, with swelling, bruising and tenderness, and the wrist may look bent [[1]](#source-1). While you wait to be seen, support the arm, hold an ice pack wrapped in a towel on it and take off rings or a watch before the swelling builds [[2]](#source-2).

**Go to the emergency room right away** if [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2):

- Your hand or fingers are numb, tingling, or pale
- The wrist looks badly bent or out of place, or bone is showing through the skin
- The pain is severe and getting worse, or it hurts a lot when someone gently straightens your fingers
- You have new weakness or trouble bending your thumb or index finger

Do not drive yourself: ask someone to take you or call for an ambulance [[2]](#source-2).

## Cast or surgery?

If the broken pieces stay in a good position, a cast is usually enough. Surgery with a plate and screws, pins or an external frame may be needed when the bone cannot be held in place in a cast or has shifted out of position [[1]](#source-1).

For older adults, trials have questioned how much difference surgery makes in the long run:

| Trial | Who | Compared | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arora, 2011 [[3]](#source-3) | 73 people aged 65 and older with a displaced, unstable fracture | Plate vs. cast | Better function early with the plate, but no difference in pain, motion or function at 6 and 12 months. Grip was stronger with the plate, and complications were more common with surgery (13 vs. 5) |
| WRIST, 2021 [[4]](#source-4) | 304 people aged 60 and older with an unstable fracture | Plate, external frame or pins (assigned at random) vs. cast (chosen by the patient) | No meaningful difference in hand function or pain at 24 months |
| DRAFFT, 2014 [[5]](#source-5) | 461 adults whose fracture needed surgery | Pins through the skin vs. plate | No meaningful difference in wrist function at 3, 6 or 12 months |

In the WRIST trial, the bone healed in a less-than-ideal position much more often with a cast (59% vs. 8% to 17% with surgery), yet that did not translate into worse hand function at 2 years [[4]](#source-4):

The authors suggested the choice should rest on each person’s needs and recovery goals rather than on long-term results alone [[4]](#source-4). Younger, more active people and complex fractures are a different discussion, so the decision belongs with your surgeon.

## The recovery timeline

| Stage | Typical timing |
| --- | --- |
| Cast removed (if no surgery) | About 4 to 6 weeks after the fracture [[1]](#source-1) |
| Usual recovery | About 6 to 12 weeks, longer for a severe injury [[2]](#source-2) |
| Light activity, such as swimming or lower-body gym work | 1 to 2 months after the cast comes off or after surgery [[1]](#source-1) |
| Healed enough for all wrist activities | About 3 months [[1]](#source-1) |
| Demanding sports, such as skiing or football | 3 to 6 months after the injury [[1]](#source-1) |
| Full recovery | Up to 1 year, with some aching during hard activity being normal [[1]](#source-1) |

Almost everyone has some stiffness. It usually improves a lot in the first month or two after the cast comes off or after surgery, and it can keep improving for at least 2 years. Lasting stiffness is more likely after severe injuries, in people over 50 and in people with arthritis, but it is usually mild [[1]](#source-1).

## What rehab really adds

Your arm will feel stiff and weak when the cast comes off, and a physical therapist can help [[2]](#source-2). Therapy can start a few days to a few weeks after surgery, or after the final cast is removed, if your doctor recommends it [[1]](#source-1).

The research on how much supervised therapy adds is honest about its limits. A Cochrane review of 26 trials with 1,269 people rated all the evidence as low or very low quality and could not say which rehab approach works best [[6]](#source-6). A few findings stand out:

- **A good home program is the core.** In four trials, routine sessions with a therapist on top of clear home-exercise instructions made no clinically important difference [[6]](#source-6). After a plate, two trials favored a structured home program started with instruction or coaching [[6]](#source-6).
- **Some guidance beats none.** In one small trial, a single physical therapy session focused on advice and a home program improved function compared with no help after the cast came off [[6]](#source-6).
- **Moving early helps.** Across upper-limb fractures, there was moderate evidence that starting exercise earlier, with a shorter period of immobilization, improves function compared with waiting longer [[7]](#source-7).
- **Passive treatments add little.** Passive joint mobilization, ice, whirlpool and similar single treatments showed no clinically important benefit compared with no treatment [[6]](#source-6).

In practice, that means rehab should focus on teaching you the right exercises, progressing them and checking how you are healing. Keep in mind that the trials mostly left out people with serious complications from the fracture or its treatment [[6]](#source-6), so if your recovery is not going as expected, closer follow-up makes sense.

## While you are in a cast

- Keep your hand raised above your elbow when you can, on a pillow at night [[2]](#source-2)
- Keep the cast dry, and do not push anything under it to scratch [[2]](#source-2)
- Do not drive or lift heavy things until you are told it is safe [[2]](#source-2)
- Tell your doctor if you cannot fully move your fingers within 24 hours because of pain or swelling [[1]](#source-1)

## Warning signs during recovery

Rare but serious problems include rising pressure in the forearm (compartment syndrome) and sudden pressure on the nerve at the wrist (acute carpal tunnel syndrome). **Severe, ongoing pain that does not get better** can be a sign of complex regional pain syndrome, which needs treatment, so do not just wait it out [[1]](#source-1).

## A broken wrist can be a warning about your bones

A wrist broken in a minor fall can be a fragility fracture, often the first sign of weak bones. One fragility fracture almost doubles the risk of another, with the highest risk in the first year or two, so your doctor may recommend a bone density test [[1]](#source-1).

## The takeaway

- A broken wrist is one of the most common fractures, from simple falls to car crashes.
- Numb or pale fingers, a badly bent wrist or severe, worsening pain need emergency care.
- In adults 60 and older, a cast and surgery led to similar hand function at 1 to 2 years in trials, but the right choice depends on the fracture and your goals.
- Most wrists heal enough for daily activity in about 3 months, and full recovery can take up to a year.
- A well-taught home exercise program and early movement matter more than passive treatments.
