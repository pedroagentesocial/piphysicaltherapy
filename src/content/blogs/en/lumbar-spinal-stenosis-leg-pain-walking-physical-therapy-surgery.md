You set out for a walk and, after a block or two, your legs start to ache, burn, tingle or feel heavy. You stop, lean forward or sit on a bench, and within a few minutes it eases enough to keep going. Leaning on a shopping cart helps, and riding a stationary bike may feel fine [[1]](#source-1).

That pattern is typical of lumbar spinal stenosis: a narrowing of the spaces in the lower spine that leaves less room for the nerves heading to the legs. It is common in older adults, and there is more you can do about it than many people expect.

## What is happening in the spine

The spinal canal and the openings where nerves leave it can narrow over time. Age-related changes in the spine are the most common cause [[2]](#source-2), and stenosis happens most often in adults over 60, in men and women equally. A smaller group of people are born with a narrow canal and tend to notice symptoms between ages 30 and 50 [[1]](#source-1).

Position matters. Sitting or bending the lower back forward opens up the spinal canal a little, which is why those positions bring relief [[2]](#source-2). Standing upright and walking do the opposite, so symptoms build the longer you are on your feet.

## Common symptoms

- Pain, cramping or numbness in the lower back, buttocks, thighs or calves, on one side or both [[10]](#source-10)
- Legs that feel heavy or weak after walking a certain distance, often 1 or 2 blocks [[1]](#source-1)
- Relief within minutes of sitting or leaning forward [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2)
- Symptoms that usually worsen slowly over time rather than suddenly [[10]](#source-10)

Leg pain with walking can also come from poor circulation in the leg arteries, and the two problems can look alike. A clinician can help tell them apart with an exam and, when needed, other tests.

## Does it get worse over time?

This is often the first worry, and the research is reassuring. In a Swedish study, 32 people with stenosis who had no treatment were followed for about 4 years. Symptoms were unchanged in 70%, better in 15% and worse in 15%, and the authors found no sign of severe deterioration [[3]](#source-3).

A larger study followed 146 people with moderate symptoms who had not been advised to have surgery, for a median of 3.3 years. Leg pain improved in 32%, stayed the same in 55% and got worse in 13%. Pain and quality of life improved slightly on average, although walking ability did not, and only 7% went on to have the decision against surgery revisited [[4]](#source-4). The one group that did not improve on its own had very severe narrowing on imaging [[4]](#source-4).

In other words, for most people stenosis is a slow, fairly stable condition. That gives time to try nonsurgical care first, unless warning signs appear.

## Physical therapy or surgery?

The most direct comparison is a U.S. trial of 169 people aged 50 or older who had stenosis and had already agreed to surgery. They were randomly assigned to decompression surgery or a physical therapy program [[5]](#source-5).

| Trial | Who took part | What was compared | Main result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Delitto, 2015 [[5]](#source-5) | 169 surgical candidates aged 50+ | Surgery vs. physical therapy | Similar physical function at 2 years |
| Schneider, 2019 [[7]](#source-7) | 259 adults over 60 | Physical therapy and chiropractic care with individualized exercise vs. medical care vs. group exercise classes | Individualized care best at 2 months; no differences at 6 months |
| Minetama, 2019 [[8]](#source-8) | 86 people with stenosis | Supervised physical therapy vs. home exercise | Supervised therapy better at 6 weeks for symptoms, walking and pain |
| Friedly, 2014 [[9]](#source-9) | 400 people with moderate to severe leg pain | Steroid plus numbing medicine vs. numbing medicine alone, by epidural injection | Minimal or no added benefit from the steroid at 6 weeks |

After 2 years, physical function had improved by an average of 22.4 points after surgery and 19.2 points after physical therapy, a difference too small to matter [[5]](#source-5). There is an important detail: 57% of people assigned to physical therapy crossed over and had surgery during the study. Even when the researchers used methods that account for those crossovers, the two approaches came out similar [[5]](#source-5).

The trial had no group without treatment, so it cannot say how much of the improvement came from each approach itself [[5]](#source-5). Its authors concluded that patients and clinicians should make the decision together, with a full picture of the evidence for both surgical and nonsurgical options [[5]](#source-5).

Data from SPORT, a large U.S. spine study, point the same way. Among 244 people with stenosis managed without surgery, the 37% who started physical therapy within 6 weeks were less likely to have surgery within a year (21% vs. 33%) and had slightly better physical function at 6 months and 1 year. There was no difference in pain or disability scores, and because this was not a randomized comparison, it shows an association rather than proof [[6]](#source-6). The lead author is a physical therapy researcher at the University of Utah [[6]](#source-6).

## What kind of physical therapy helps most

Not all exercise programs are equal. In a trial of 259 adults over 60, three approaches were compared over 6 weeks [[7]](#source-7):

- **Medical care:** medications and/or epidural injections from a physician
- **Group exercise:** classes at senior community centers led by fitness instructors
- **Individualized care:** spinal mobilization, stretching and strength training from physical therapists and chiropractors

At 2 months, the individualized approach improved symptoms, function and walking more than either medical care or group classes. By 6 months, the three groups were no longer different, and all three were linked with better walking over the long term [[7]](#source-7).

A Japanese trial of 86 people found a similar pattern. Supervised physical therapy twice a week for 6 weeks, including manual therapy, tailored stretching and strengthening, cycling and treadmill walking with part of the body weight supported, beat a home exercise program for symptoms, function, walking distance, leg pain and daily step counts [[8]](#source-8).

Taken together, the trials suggest that a program tailored to you, with hands-on guidance at first, brings faster relief than generic exercise.

## What about injections?

Epidural steroid injections are widely used for stenosis. In a double-blind trial of 400 people with moderate to severe leg pain, an injection of steroid plus a numbing medicine offered minimal or no extra benefit at 6 weeks compared with the numbing medicine alone [[9]](#source-9). OrthoInfo notes that these injections may ease numbness but not weakness, and advises no more than three a year [[1]](#source-1).

Anti-inflammatory medicines can relieve pain at first, but they can cause stomach ulcers and kidney problems when used for a long time [[1]](#source-1). Nonsurgical treatments do not widen the spinal canal, but many people find they relieve symptoms [[1]](#source-1).

## What you can do now

- **Use the positions that help.** Sitting breaks, leaning forward on a cart or walker, and cycling let you stay active with less pain [[1]](#source-1).
- **Keep walking, in short bouts.** Walk to the point where symptoms start, rest briefly and continue. Walking capacity often improves with a structured program [[7]](#source-7) [[8]](#source-8).
- **Build strength and flexibility.** Stretching and strengthening for the back, core and hips are standard parts of nonsurgical care [[1]](#source-1).
- **Get a tailored plan.** In the trials, individualized and supervised programs worked better in the first weeks than generic classes or exercising alone at home [[7]](#source-7) [[8]](#source-8).

Surgery remains a reasonable option for people whose quality of life is poor because of pain and weakness despite nonsurgical care [[1]](#source-1).

## When to get medical help

**Call 911 or go to the emergency room** if you suddenly lose control of your bladder or bowels, or have numbness in the groin or genital area along with leg weakness. These can be signs of cauda equina syndrome, a rare emergency that needs prompt treatment [[1]](#source-1).

**See a clinician promptly** if you have increasing trouble with balance when walking, numbness or weakness in a leg that keeps getting worse, or difficulty lifting the front of your foot [[1]](#source-1) [[10]](#source-10).

**Make an appointment** if you have symptoms of spinal stenosis, so the cause of your leg pain can be confirmed [[10]](#source-10).

## The takeaway

- Lumbar spinal stenosis typically causes leg pain, heaviness or tingling with walking or standing that eases when you sit or lean forward.
- For most people it changes slowly, and many stay the same or improve over several years.
- In a trial of people who had already agreed to surgery, physical therapy led to similar physical function at 2 years.
- Individualized, supervised physical therapy improved symptoms and walking faster than group classes, medical care or home exercise alone.
- Steroid injections added little in a large trial. Sudden bladder or bowel changes or numbness in the groin need emergency care.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If your legs give out on walks and feel better when you sit, an evaluation can confirm what is going on and build a program to help you walk farther with less pain.
