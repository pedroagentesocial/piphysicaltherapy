A fall on an outstretched arm, a tackle, a hard block at the net. The shoulder pops out, and the pain is immediate. Once it has been put back in place, two questions usually follow: how long until it feels normal, and will it happen again?

The shoulder is the most mobile joint in the body, and that freedom of movement makes it easy to dislocate. Most of the time the top of the arm bone slides forward out of the socket [[1]](#source-1). Here is what the research says about recovery, the risk of another dislocation and the role of physical therapy and surgery.

## Who it happens to

In the United States, about 24 shoulder dislocations per 100,000 people a year are seen in emergency rooms. Nearly 72% happen in men, the rate peaks between ages 20 and 29, and almost half occur during sports or recreation [[3]](#source-3). Falls are the most common cause [[3]](#source-3).

A dislocation is an emergency injury. The shoulder is usually put back in place by a doctor, a step called a closed reduction [[1]](#source-1). Do not try to put it back yourself, and do not drive yourself to the hospital [[2]](#source-2).

## More than a dislocation, especially after 60

A dislocation can come with other injuries. In a study of 3,633 people with a dislocated shoulder, average age 47.6, 13.5% had nerve damage after the shoulder was put back, and 33.4% had a rotator cuff tear or a fracture of the bony bump at the top of the arm bone [[4]](#source-4). Those injuries were more common in people 60 and older, especially women who dislocated their shoulder in a simple fall [[4]](#source-4).

That is why an older adult whose shoulder stays weak after a dislocation needs a careful check of the rotator cuff and the nerves, not just time in a sling.

## What recovery looks like

You will usually wear a sling for the first few weeks, and recovery generally takes 6 to 12 weeks. Returning to some sports can take up to 16 weeks or longer [[2]](#source-2). Physical therapy helps restore the shoulder's range of motion and strengthen its muscles [[1]](#source-1).

In the first days, the NHS suggests holding an ice pack wrapped in a towel to the shoulder for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours and supporting the arm in the sling [[2]](#source-2).

## Will it dislocate again?

For young, active people, the risk of another dislocation is high. In a study of 252 people aged 15 to 35 treated with a sling and then physical therapy, the shoulder became unstable again in 55.7% within 2 years and 66.8% within 5 years [[5]](#source-5). Young men were most at risk, women had a much lower risk, and 86.7% of repeat problems showed up within the first 2 years [[5]](#source-5).

The longest follow-up comes from Sweden, where 229 shoulders in people aged 12 to 40 were treated without surgery after a first dislocation and followed for 25 years [[6]](#source-6).

Age made a large difference. Leaving aside shoulders that also had a small fracture at the time of injury, 38% of those injured at ages 12 to 25 eventually had stabilizing surgery, compared with 18% of those injured at 26 to 40 [[6]](#source-6). Even in the youngest group, half of the shoulders had not dislocated again or had become stable over time [[6]](#source-6).

A review of the trials reached a similar conclusion: about half of the people managed with physical therapy after a first dislocation did not dislocate again [[9]](#source-9).

## Does a longer time in a sling help?

It seems not. Several studies have tested how long and in what position the arm should be immobilized:

| Question | Study | What it found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 week or less vs. 3 weeks or more in a sling (people under 30) | Paterson, 2010 [[7]](#source-7) | Repeat instability in 41% vs. 37%, no meaningful difference |
| Sling in the usual position vs. a brace that turns the arm outward, for 3 weeks | Liavaag, 2011 [[8]](#source-8) | Repeat dislocation in 24.7% vs. 30.8%, no benefit from the brace |
| Tying the arm to the body vs. no immobilization | Hovelius, 2008 [[6]](#source-6) | Immobilization did not change the outlook |

A review of the evidence concluded that there is no benefit to wearing a sling for longer than 1 week in younger patients, and that being under 30 at the time of the injury strongly predicts another dislocation [[7]](#source-7). A sling is still useful for comfort in the early days [[2]](#source-2), but keeping the arm still for longer does not appear to protect the shoulder.

## When surgery makes sense

Surgery may be needed if the shoulder cannot be put back in place or has dislocated several times [[1]](#source-1). For a first-time dislocation, the question is whether to operate early to prevent the next one.

Repairing the torn labrum, the rim of cartilage around the socket, does reduce the risk. A review of 22 randomized trials found moderate-quality evidence that labrum repair lowered the risk of future dislocation [[9]](#source-9). In a trial of 88 people aged 35 or younger after a first dislocation, followed for an average of 14 years, 47% of those who had only a joint washout dislocated again, compared with 12% of those who had arthroscopic labrum repair [[10]](#source-10).

The trade-off is that about half of people never dislocate again without surgery [[9]](#source-9), so early surgery means some people have an operation they would not have needed. The review's authors suggested that surgery could be considered if chronic instability develops [[9]](#source-9). The decision is worth discussing with an orthopedic surgeon, especially for young people, who face the highest risk [[5]](#source-5) [[6]](#source-6).

## How physical therapy helps

Whether or not you have surgery, rehabilitation follows the same basic path:

- **Calm the shoulder** in the first days with the sling and ice [[2]](#source-2).
- **Restore range of motion** step by step [[1]](#source-1).
- **Strengthen the muscles** around the shoulder [[1]](#source-1).
- **Return to sport in stages**, with your physical therapist or doctor advising when it is safe to drive and play again [[2]](#source-2).

Some people return to full activity, while others keep a sense of instability and may use a brace during sport [[1]](#source-1).

## When to get medical help

**Go to the emergency room or call 911** if you have injured your shoulder and cannot move your arm, your shoulder looks out of place or has changed shape, or it is painful, bruised or swollen [[2]](#source-2). Do not try to put it back in yourself, and do not drive [[2]](#source-2).

**See a clinician** if your arm or shoulder feels numb, weak or hard to lift after the shoulder has been put back, since nerve and rotator cuff injuries are more common than once thought, especially after age 60 [[4]](#source-4).

**Get checked again** if your shoulder slips, feels loose or dislocates again, since repeated dislocations can lead to chronic instability [[1]](#source-1).

## The takeaway

- Most dislocated shoulders slip forward out of the socket and need to be put back by a doctor. Do not try it yourself.
- Recovery usually takes 6 to 12 weeks, and some sports take longer.
- Young people, especially young men, have the highest risk of another dislocation, mostly in the first 2 years.
- A longer time in a sling, or a special brace, did not lower that risk in trials.
- About half of people never dislocate again without surgery. Early labrum repair lowers the risk and is worth discussing for young people at high risk.
