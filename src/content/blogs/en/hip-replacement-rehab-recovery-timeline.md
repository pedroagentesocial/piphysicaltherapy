A total hip replacement swaps a damaged hip joint for an artificial one. The operation is one step; rehab is what turns a new joint into a hip that works well for daily life. Here is what to expect, based on the surgeons’ patient guides and the research.

## The recovery timeline

Every surgeon has their own protocol, and your age, health and surgical approach all affect the pace. A typical path looks like this:

| When | What usually happens |
| --- | --- |
| Day of surgery or the next day | You start walking with a walker or crutches. Some people go home the same day; others stay 1 to 3 days [[1]](#source-1) [[4]](#source-4) |
| First 1 to 2 weeks | You will need someone with you at home [[3]](#source-3). Stitches or staples come out at about 2 weeks [[1]](#source-1) |
| 2 to 4 weeks | Most people no longer need the walker or crutches [[3]](#source-3) |
| 3 to 6 weeks | Most normal light daily activities are possible again [[1]](#source-1) |
| About 6 weeks | Many people return to work, depending on the job, and the NHS advises waiting at least this long to drive [[4]](#source-4) |
| 3 months and beyond | Sport and more demanding activities, with full recovery taking several months [[4]](#source-4) [[16]](#source-16) |

*Sources: AAOS OrthoInfo [[1]](#source-1); MedlinePlus [[3]](#source-3); NHS [[4]](#source-4); Magan et al. [[16]](#source-16).*

## Moving early, and getting ready beforehand

**Getting up soon after surgery helps.** A review of 5 trials of hip and knee replacement found that getting people sitting out of bed within 24 hours and walking within 48 hours shortened the hospital stay by about 1.8 days, with no increase in complications [[5]](#source-5).

**Exercise before surgery gives a small boost.** For hip replacement, exercise and education before surgery led to slightly less pain and better function afterward, though the effects were small to moderate [[6]](#source-6).

## Home exercise or supervised physical therapy?

Rehab exercises after hip replacement clearly help: a review of 5 trials found physical therapy improved hip strength, walking speed and step rate, with similar results in outpatient and home-based programs [[7]](#source-7).

Whether you need formal visits depends on you. In one trial of 120 relatively healthy patients going straight home, a self-directed home program did as well as formal physical therapy over 10 weeks [[8]](#source-8). Many people crossed over between groups, though, and the patients were carefully selected [[8]](#source-8). The AAOS hip osteoarthritis guideline supports either formal physical therapy or unsupervised home exercise after surgery, at a moderate level [[9]](#source-9). Talk with your surgeon and therapist about which approach fits you.

Whatever you choose, keep doing your prescribed exercises for at least 2 months after surgery [[2]](#source-2).

## Do you still need hip precautions?

After the traditional posterior (back) approach, patients have long been told to avoid movements such as bending the hip past 90 degrees or crossing the legs, usually for the first 6 weeks [[1]](#source-1) [[4]](#source-4). Trials have now tested whether these rules prevent dislocation:

| Study | People | With precautions | Without precautions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dietz, 2019 [[11]](#source-11) | 313 | 1.3% dislocated | 0.7% dislocated |
| Tetreault, 2020 [[12]](#source-12) | 585 hips | 1.03% | 0.68% |
| Yadav, 2026 [[13]](#source-13) | 1,133 | 6 dislocations | 4 dislocations |
| Review of 4 trials, 2023 [[10]](#source-10) | 1,616 | 1.2% | 0.6% |

*None of these differences was statistically significant. All studies used the posterior approach.*

A 2023 review concluded that the evidence does not support routinely prescribing hip precautions after the posterior approach [[10]](#source-10), and the 2026 trial found slightly better hip scores without them at 6 weeks and 3 to 6 months [[13]](#source-13). There are caveats: dislocations are rare, so the trials are too small to rule out a small difference [[10]](#source-10), and in a Danish registry the rate was slightly, though not significantly, higher after precautions were dropped (3.5% vs. 2.9% at 3 months) [[14]](#source-14).

**The bottom line: follow your own surgeon’s instructions.** Precautions depend on your surgical approach, and the AAOS notes that most people can do most of the restricted movements after 6 to 8 weeks [[2]](#source-2).

## Driving, sport and work

**Driving:** the AAOS says it is generally safe once you are off opioid pain medicine and your strength and reflexes have returned [[2]](#source-2); the NHS advises waiting at least 6 weeks and checking with your doctor [[4]](#source-4). Brake reaction time can return to normal as early as 2 weeks after a right hip replacement [[15]](#source-15), but reaction time is only one part of being safe to drive.

**Sport:** people return gradually over months.

Low-impact activities such as swimming once the wound has healed, golf, cycling and doubles tennis are usually encouraged, while singles tennis, jogging, basketball and skiing are higher impact [[2]](#source-2). Ask your surgeon before taking up high-impact sport.

**Work:** return can take from several weeks to several months, depending on the job [[2]](#source-2). In a review, 69% of people returned to work [[17]](#source-17).

## How long will the new hip last?

A long time for most people. National registries suggest about 58% of hip replacements last 25 years [[18]](#source-18).

## When to call your surgeon

Call your surgeon if you have [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3):

- Pain or tenderness in your calf that is not related to the incision, or new swelling in your thigh, calf, ankle or foot
- A fever above 101°F (38.3°C) or chills
- Redness, increasing pain or drainage from the incision
- A sudden increase in hip pain, or pain your medicine does not control
- Trouble walking or moving the hip, a fall or injury to the leg, or a feeling that the hip has slipped out of place

**Call 911** for sudden shortness of breath or chest pain, which can mean a blood clot has traveled to the lungs [[1]](#source-1) [[3]](#source-3).

Serious complications such as joint infection affect less than 2% of patients, and dislocation is uncommon, with the risk greatest in the first few months [[1]](#source-1).

## The takeaway

- Most people walk within a day, drop the walker in 2 to 4 weeks and resume light activities by 3 to 6 weeks.
- Early movement and some exercise before surgery help recovery.
- Home exercise can work well for healthy people; supervised therapy helps those who need more support.
- Routine hip precautions may not be necessary after the posterior approach, but follow your surgeon’s plan.
- Calf pain, swelling, fever, wound drainage or breathing trouble need prompt attention.
