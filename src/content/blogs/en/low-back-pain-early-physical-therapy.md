If your back hurts, you have a lot of company. In 2019, **39% of US adults had back pain** at some point in the previous three months, according to the National Center for Health Statistics [[1]](#source-1). Worldwide, low back pain affected an estimated **619 million people in 2020**, and that number is projected to reach **843 million by 2050** as the population grows and ages [[2]](#source-2).

The good news: most back pain is not dangerous, and the research on what helps is clearer than many people expect.

Women reported it slightly more often than men (40.6% vs. 37.2%) [[1]](#source-1).

## Do you need an MRI or X-ray first?

Usually, no. A meta-analysis of six randomized trials with 1,804 patients found that imaging people with low back pain who had no signs of a serious underlying condition **did not improve pain or function**, in the short term or after 6 to 12 months [[3]](#source-3). The authors concluded that clinicians should not order routine, immediate imaging for these patients [[3]](#source-3).

The American College of Physicians puts it this way: imaging is indicated only for severe or progressive nerve problems or when a serious condition is suspected; otherwise routine imaging is "not associated with clinically meaningful benefits but can lead to harms" [[4]](#source-4). Scans often show age-related changes that are common in people without any pain, which can lead to worry and more procedures without changing the plan.

That is why a good first visit focuses on a careful history and physical exam. If anything suggests your pain needs imaging or a doctor’s evaluation, a physical therapist will refer you.

## What the guidelines recommend first

Major guidelines put non-drug, movement-based care at the front of the line:

- **Recent pain (acute or subacute).** The American College of Physicians notes that most people improve over time regardless of treatment, and recommends starting with options such as superficial heat, massage, acupuncture or spinal manipulation [[5]](#source-5).
- **Chronic pain (lasting 12 weeks or more).** The same guideline recommends starting with non-drug treatments, with **exercise** at the top of its list, along with multidisciplinary rehabilitation, tai chi, yoga, motor control exercise and cognitive behavioral therapy, among others [[5]](#source-5).
- **Opioids** should be considered only after other treatments have failed, and only if the benefits outweigh the risks [[5]](#source-5).

The World Health Organization’s 2023 guideline on chronic low back pain points the same way. It recommends education for self-care, exercise programs, some physical therapies such as spinal manipulation and massage, and psychological therapies. For most people, it recommends **against** lumbar braces and belts, traction, and opioid pain medicines [[6]](#source-6).

A physical therapist’s job is to put those pieces together for you: explain what is going on, choose the right exercises and hands-on care, and help you stay active while you recover.

## Why timing matters

A study led by University of Utah researchers followed 32,070 patients who saw a primary care provider for a new episode of low back pain. Those who started physical therapy **within 14 days** were compared with those who started later (within 90 days) [[7]](#source-7). Over the next 18 months, the early group had markedly lower odds of needing more care:

| Over the next 18 months | Early PT vs. delayed PT (odds ratio) |
| --- | --- |
| Advanced imaging (MRI or CT) | 0.34 |
| Additional physician visits | 0.26 |
| Lumbar injections | 0.42 |
| Lumbar surgery | 0.45 |
| Opioid prescriptions | 0.78 |

*An odds ratio below 1 means lower odds in the early group. Source: Fritz et al., Spine, 2012 [[7]](#source-7).*

Total back-pain medical costs were also **about $2,736 lower** per patient in the early group [[7]](#source-7). A larger study of more than 750,000 patients in the Military Health System found the same pattern: early physical therapy that followed clinical guidelines was linked to less use of imaging, injections, surgery and opioids, and **60% lower** total back-pain costs [[8]](#source-8).

These were observational studies, so they show a strong association rather than proof of cause. Still, the message from both is consistent: when physical therapy is part of the plan, starting sooner tends to go better than waiting.

## When back pain is an emergency

Most back pain is not serious, but some symptoms need medical attention right away. **Call 911 or go to the emergency room** if you have back pain with any of the following [[9]](#source-9):

- Weakness, numbness or tingling in **both legs**
- Loss of feeling around the genitals or buttocks
- New trouble controlling your bladder or bowels
- Chest pain
- Pain that started after a serious accident, such as a car crash or a fall

See a doctor promptly if your back pain comes with fever or feeling generally unwell, unexplained weight loss, or pain that is severe and getting worse quickly or that does not ease with rest [[9]](#source-9).

## The takeaway

- Back pain is common, and most of it gets better.
- For most people without red flags, an MRI first does not lead to better recovery.
- Guidelines recommend movement-based care, education and exercise before medicines like opioids.
- Starting physical therapy early is linked to less imaging, fewer procedures and lower costs.

In Utah you can start physical therapy without a referral. If your back has been bothering you, a first evaluation is a good place to begin.
