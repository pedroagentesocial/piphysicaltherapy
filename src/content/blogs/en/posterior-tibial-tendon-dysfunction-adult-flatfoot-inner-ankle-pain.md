It starts as an ache along the inside of the ankle after a long walk. Over months, the ankle swells a little. Then you notice that one foot looks flatter than the other, and going up on your toes on that side is hard or impossible.

That pattern is typical of posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, a painful tendon problem that goes hand in hand with a slowly flattening foot [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2). It is common, especially in women in midlife, and it is often missed [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3). Because it tends to get worse over time, early treatment matters [[1]](#source-1). The encouraging news is that in the early stages, many people do well with orthoses and a structured exercise program [[6]](#source-6) [[10]](#source-10).

## What goes wrong

The problem lies in the tendon of the tibialis posterior, a muscle in the lower leg. When the tendon is painful and not working well, people lose strength in turning the foot inward, struggle with heel raises and have a lower arch than people without the condition [[2]](#source-2).

The condition goes by many names: posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, adult acquired flatfoot and others. In 2020, an international group of foot and ankle surgeons proposed a new name, progressive collapsing foot deformity, because many but not all cases involve a damaged tendon and the deformity can also involve the midfoot and ankle [[4]](#source-4).

## Who gets it

Researchers describe it as a painful, progressive tendon problem that is reported to be most common in middle-aged, overweight women [[2]](#source-2). In a survey of women over 40 at an English general practice, about 3.3% had symptoms of early-stage posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, and none of them had been diagnosed, despite having typical, long-lasting symptoms [[3]](#source-3).

Other risk factors include [[1]](#source-1):

- Overuse of the tendon from poor foot mechanics or sports
- Excess body weight
- Age-related changes that make the tendon less elastic
- A previous injury, such as an ankle sprain
- Conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes and high blood pressure

## Signs to look for

Symptoms vary and tend to progress [[1]](#source-1):

- Pain and swelling along the inside of the ankle
- A foot that gradually becomes flatter
- Trouble rising up onto tiptoe on that side
- Pain on the outside of the ankle

People with the condition also report more stiffness, more difficulty because of their feet and more limits on their social lives than people without it [[2]](#source-2).

### The single-leg heel raise

The simplest test is standing on the affected leg and rising up onto your toes. In a study of 52 people with pain on the inside of the foot and ankle, two physical therapists examined everyone separately. The single-leg heel raise was the test they agreed on most often, and the one most closely linked to changes in the tendon on ultrasound [[5]](#source-5). The study also found that what clinicians felt and saw did not always match what the ultrasound showed, so the exam and how the tendon handles load are central to the diagnosis [[5]](#source-5).

## What nonsurgical treatment includes

The Royal Orthopaedic Hospital in the UK describes the main nonsurgical options [[1]](#source-1):

- **Orthotics or a brace** to improve how the foot works and rest the tendon
- **A boot or cast** to rest the tendon, but usually only for a short time, because wearing a boot too long can weaken the tendon
- **Physical therapy,** with strengthening exercises it describes as "extremely important," which may prevent the need for surgery
- **Weight loss,** a healthy lifestyle and pain medication when advised

Steroid injections may ease pain but are not a cure and can cause side effects, including tendon rupture [[1]](#source-1).

## What the studies show

| Study | What it found |
| --- | --- |
| US cohort, 47 people, stages I and II (2006) | An orthosis plus a structured exercise program: 83% had a successful result after a median of 10 therapy visits over 4 months, and 11% needed surgery [[6]](#source-6) |
| US trial, 36 people, stages I and II (2009) | Orthoses and stretching helped. Adding strengthening, especially eccentric (lowering) exercise, reduced pain and improved function further [[7]](#source-7) |
| US trial, 39 people, stage II (2015) | Both groups improved over 12 weeks, but a moderate home strengthening program added little to an orthosis and stretching [[8]](#source-8) |
| Review of 4 trials, 186 people (2021) | Orthoses with custom arch support reduced pain more than flat insoles, and adding exercise seemed to help [[9]](#source-9) |
| US follow-up, 32 people, stage II (2008) | With an ankle brace, about 70% were brace-free and had avoided surgery 7 to 10 years later [[10]](#source-10) |

None of these studies is large, and two of them followed a single group without a comparison group [[6]](#source-6) [[10]](#source-10). But together they point the same way: in the early stages, most people can be treated without surgery, at least for years.

### What the exercise looked like

The 2006 program that worked for most people was demanding. It combined a short hinged ankle brace or foot orthosis with high-repetition strengthening, plantar flexion work (pointing the foot down, as in heel raises) and an intensive home program that included calf stretching [[6]](#source-6). To finish, people had to show less than a 10% strength gap between legs, do 50 single-leg heel raises with little or no pain, walk 100 feet on their toes and tolerate 200 repetitions of the home exercises [[6]](#source-6).

The 2015 trial helps explain why intensity matters. A home strengthening program of moderate intensity added little to an orthosis and stretching [[8]](#source-8). The authors of the 2021 review also called for more research into individualized orthoses and high-intensity, monitored exercise programs [[9]](#source-9).

## When surgery comes up

An orthopedic surgeon may recommend surgery when nonsurgical treatment fails or walking is very painful [[1]](#source-1). The operation can be extensive and often requires about 3 months in a cast [[1]](#source-1). In the studies above, between 11% and 15% of people treated without surgery eventually had an operation [[6]](#source-6) [[10]](#source-10).

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can help at each stage:

- Check the single-leg heel raise, arch height and strength of the muscles that turn the foot inward [[2]](#source-2) [[5]](#source-5)
- Work with your doctor or podiatrist on an orthosis or brace that supports the arch [[9]](#source-9)
- Build a progressive strengthening program that includes heel raises and eccentric exercise, with enough intensity to make a difference [[6]](#source-6) [[7]](#source-7)
- Add calf stretching [[6]](#source-6) [[7]](#source-7)
- Help you wean off a boot early enough to avoid weakening the tendon [[1]](#source-1)
- Track your progress with clear goals, like the number of heel raises you can do with little or no pain [[6]](#source-6)

## The takeaway

- Pain and swelling along the inside of the ankle, a flattening arch and trouble rising onto your toes on one side can be posterior tibial tendon dysfunction.
- It is common in women in midlife and often goes undiagnosed. Because it tends to worsen, early treatment matters.
- The single-leg heel raise is a simple, reliable test.
- In the early stages, orthoses or a brace plus a structured, progressive exercise program help most people avoid surgery.
- Surgery is an option when nonsurgical care fails, but it is a big operation with a long recovery.
