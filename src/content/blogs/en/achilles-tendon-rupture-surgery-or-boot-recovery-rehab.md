A sprint for a ball, a jump at the net, a push off the line in a pickup game. Many people say it felt like someone kicked them in the back of the heel. When they turn around, nobody is there.

The Achilles is the largest tendon in the body. It connects the calf muscles to the heel bone and lets you push off and rise onto your toes [[1]](#source-1). When it tears all the way through, the first big question is whether to have surgery. The good news is that large trials have studied this question closely. Here is what they found.

## How to tell it is a rupture

A torn Achilles often comes with a popping sound, followed by pain and swelling near the heel. You may not be able to walk normally on the leg or stand on the toes of that foot [[1]](#source-1). Tears usually happen when the calf is stretched too quickly, such as landing from a jump or making a sharp cut in sports [[1]](#source-1).

A doctor can usually diagnose it with an exam. One common check is the Thompson test: you lie face down and the doctor squeezes your calf. If the foot does not move, the tendon is likely torn [[1]](#source-1). An ultrasound or MRI can help when the picture is less clear [[1]](#source-1).

Some things make the tendon weaker and more likely to tear, including steroid injections around the tendon, certain antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin, and conditions such as diabetes, inflammatory arthritis and obesity [[1]](#source-1).

**Get it checked within days, not weeks.** If surgery is chosen, it goes better when it is done soon after the injury, because the tendon ends begin to shorten and scar over time [[1]](#source-1).

## It is becoming more common

In Oulu, Finland, the rate of Achilles ruptures rose from 2.1 to 21.5 per 100,000 people a year between 1979 and 2011, in every age group [[2]](#source-2). A Danish registry of 33,160 ruptures found about 27 to 31 per 100,000 people a year. Men had three times as many ruptures as women, the average age was about 45, and the rate kept climbing in people over 50 [[3]](#source-3).

The same registry showed something else: surgery became much less common. The rate of operations fell from 16.9 to 6.3 per 100,000 people a year, which the authors linked to a series of high-quality trials [[3]](#source-3).

## Surgery or no surgery: what the trials found

For many years, surgery was the default because it seemed to lower the risk of the tendon tearing again. The trials tell a more balanced story:

| Study | Who was studied | What it found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Willits, 2010 [[5]](#source-5) | 144 adults, average age 40, all with early weight-bearing and early movement | Re-rupture in 2 people with surgery and 3 without. No meaningful difference in strength, motion or function. 13 complications with surgery vs. 6 without |
| Ochen, 2019 [[4]](#source-4) | 29 studies, more than 15,000 patients | Re-rupture 2.3% with surgery vs. 3.9% without. Other complications 4.9% vs. 1.6%, mainly infection |
| Myhrvold, 2022 [[6]](#source-6) | 526 adults in Norway, three groups | No difference in symptoms and function at 12 months. Re-rupture 6.2% without surgery vs. 0.6% with either type of surgery |

Taken together, surgery lowers the risk of re-rupture a little, but it adds its own risks. In the review of 29 studies, the difference in re-rupture was 1.6 percentage points, while surgery raised other complications by 3.3 points [[4]](#source-4). In studies where people without surgery started early movement as part of their rehab, the re-rupture difference was no longer statistically significant [[4]](#source-4).

Despite the gap in re-ruptures, people in all three groups reported similar symptoms, physical function and physical performance a year later [[6]](#source-6).

Surgeons still debate the question. A surgeon may suggest repair for a competitive athlete or someone whose work or sport depends on jumping and pushing off [[1]](#source-1). The decision is personal and depends on your age, activity level and job [[1]](#source-1), and it is worth talking through with an orthopedic surgeon.

## Cast or boot?

If you heal without surgery, the foot is held with the toes pointed down so the tendon ends stay close together while they heal. Doctors use a boot, splint or cast, and you should plan on at least 6 weeks of immobilization [[1]](#source-1). Protocols differ on how soon you can put weight on the leg [[1]](#source-1).

The UKSTAR trial in 39 hospitals compared a traditional plaster cast with a functional boot that allowed early weight-bearing, each worn for 8 weeks. Among 540 people treated without surgery, function at 9 months was the same in both groups, and re-rupture rates were similar: 6% with the cast and 5% with the boot [[7]](#source-7). The authors concluded that early weight-bearing in a boot is a safe alternative to a cast [[7]](#source-7).

## How long recovery takes

Recovery is slow, whatever the treatment:

- **First 6 weeks or so:** boot, cast or splint, with crutches at first [[1]](#source-1).
- **The following months:** the boot is weaned off, range of motion comes back first, then strengthening is added to rebuild push-off power [[1]](#source-1).
- **About 12 months:** full recovery, although some people say it took closer to 2 years to reach all their goals [[1]](#source-1).

Most people return to their job and daily activities [[1]](#source-1). In a review of 108 studies with 6,506 patients, 80% returned to sport, although studies that used stricter definitions of return reported lower rates [[8]](#source-8).

## The calf weakness that can linger

Some loss of strength is common after a rupture, whatever the treatment [[1]](#source-1). A Swedish study tested 66 people an average of 7 years after their injury. The injured side was still weaker in every calf test, and strength had stopped improving after the 2-year mark. Even so, most people reported few symptoms and were fairly active, and there was little difference between those who had surgery and those who did not [[9]](#source-9).

The lesson is that the first 1 to 2 years of strengthening count. A tendon that tears a second time is also worth avoiding: in another Swedish study, people treated for a re-rupture reported clearly worse results on a standard Achilles questionnaire than people with a single rupture (78 vs. 89.5 out of 100) [[10]](#source-10).

## How physical therapy helps

With or without surgery, rehab follows a careful progression:

- **Protect the healing tendon** in the boot, cast or splint, following your surgeon's or doctor's plan on weight-bearing [[1]](#source-1).
- **Start movement early when it is allowed.** The trials that compared surgery with no surgery on equal terms used early weight-bearing and early ankle movement [[5]](#source-5) [[4]](#source-4).
- **Restore ankle motion step by step** as the boot is weaned off [[1]](#source-1).
- **Rebuild calf strength and endurance**, the part most likely to fall short in the long run [[9]](#source-9).
- **Return to running and sport in stages**, using tests to check readiness rather than the calendar alone [[1]](#source-1).

## When to get medical help

**Get seen urgently** if you felt a pop or a kick in the back of the heel, cannot stand on your toes, or cannot walk normally on the leg [[1]](#source-1). Early diagnosis keeps both treatment options open [[1]](#source-1).

**Call your doctor** if, during recovery, you feel a new pop, a sudden loss of push-off strength, or a gap in the tendon, since the tendon can tear again with or without surgery [[1]](#source-1) [[6]](#source-6).

**After surgery, watch the wound.** Infection and nerve injury are among the possible complications [[1]](#source-1) [[4]](#source-4).

## The takeaway

- A pop in the back of the heel and trouble rising on your toes point to an Achilles rupture. Get checked within days.
- Surgery lowers the risk of re-rupture a little but adds other risks, such as infection and nerve injury. Function a year later was similar with or without surgery in the large trials.
- Without surgery, a boot that allows early weight-bearing worked as well as a plaster cast.
- Recovery takes about a year, and some people need up to 2 years. About 80% of people return to sport.
- Calf weakness can linger for years, so steady strengthening during the first 1 to 2 years matters.
