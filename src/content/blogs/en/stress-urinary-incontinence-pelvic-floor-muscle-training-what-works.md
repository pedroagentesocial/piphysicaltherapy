A sneeze in the grocery store. A laugh with friends. A jump on the trampoline with the kids. For many women, any of these can mean a small leak, and over time it can change what you wear, where you go and whether you exercise at all.

Leaking urine is common, but it is not something you simply have to accept with age or after having children. The first treatment that guidelines recommend is not a pill or an operation. It is an exercise program for the muscles that hold urine in, and the evidence behind it is some of the strongest in rehabilitation [[4]](#source-4) [[5]](#source-5). Here is what the research shows.

## The main types of leaking

Doctors usually describe three types of urinary incontinence [[1]](#source-1):

- **Stress incontinence:** urine leaks when the bladder is under pressure, for example when you cough or laugh.
- **Urge incontinence:** urine leaks as you feel a sudden, intense need to go, or soon afterward.
- **Mixed incontinence:** a mix of both.

Stress incontinence is usually linked to weakened or damaged muscles that keep urine in, such as the pelvic floor muscles and the sphincter around the urethra [[1]](#source-1). Things that raise the risk include pregnancy and vaginal birth, obesity, a family history of incontinence and getting older. The NHS stresses that incontinence is not an inevitable part of aging [[1]](#source-1).

## How common it is

In a national US survey of nearly 2,000 women, 15.7% had moderate to severe urinary leakage [[2]](#source-2). About 1 in 4 women had at least one pelvic floor problem, such as leaking urine or stool or a bulge from prolapse. That share rose with age, from 9.7% of women aged 20 to 39 to 49.7% of women aged 80 and older, and with the number of births. Overweight and obese women also reported more problems [[2]](#source-2).

Because it is so common and so rarely discussed, many women never bring it up. The NHS advice is simple: see a doctor about any type of urinary incontinence, and do not feel embarrassed to raise it, because that visit is the first step to managing it [[1]](#source-1).

## What the guidelines recommend first

The American College of Physicians reviewed the evidence on nonsurgical treatment and made these strong recommendations for women [[4]](#source-4):

| Type of leaking | First-line treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Stress | Pelvic floor muscle training (high-quality evidence) |
| Urge | Bladder training |
| Mixed | Pelvic floor muscle training plus bladder training |
| Any type, with obesity | Weight loss and exercise |

The same guideline recommends against medications for stress incontinence, and reserves medication for urge incontinence when bladder training has not worked [[4]](#source-4). The NHS gives similar advice and adds lifestyle steps: cut back on caffeine and get your fluid intake right, since drinking too much or too little can make leaking worse [[3]](#source-3).

## What pelvic floor muscle training does

Pelvic floor muscle training, often called Kegel exercises, means repeatedly and deliberately squeezing the muscles at the base of the pelvis. A Cochrane review pooled 31 trials with 1,817 women from 14 countries that compared this training with no treatment or a fake treatment [[5]](#source-5). Among women with stress incontinence:

Women who trained were about eight times more likely to say they were cured, based on high-quality evidence [[5]](#source-5). They also had about one fewer leak a day, lost less urine on clinic pad tests and were more likely to report better quality of life [[5]](#source-5). For women with any type of incontinence, the benefit was smaller but still clear: 35% reported a cure with training versus 6% without [[5]](#source-5). The trials were mostly small to moderate in size, and most followed women for less than a year [[5]](#source-5).

## How a program works

The NHS describes the usual approach [[3]](#source-3):

1. **A specialist checks your muscles first.** A physical therapist or continence nurse confirms that you can contract your pelvic floor muscles and how strongly.
2. **You train often.** Programs should include at least 8 contractions at least 3 times a day.
3. **You keep going for at least 3 months.** If it is helping, you can carry on.

If you cannot contract the muscles at all, electrical stimulation may be suggested to help get them working [[3]](#source-3).

### Does the type of training matter?

A 2024 Cochrane review compared different ways of doing the training in 63 trials with 4,920 women [[6]](#source-6). Direct training, meaning repeated, isolated squeezes of the pelvic floor, may improve quality of life more than general exercises that do not target those muscles. Training on more days of the week may help more than training on fewer days, though that comes from a single trial [[6]](#source-6). Most other comparisons, such as using a resistance device or not, had weak or uncertain evidence [[6]](#source-6). About 6% of women in trials that tracked side effects had one, almost always discomfort or discharge from a device placed in the vagina or rectum [[6]](#source-6).

### Alone or in a group?

You do not necessarily need one-on-one sessions for every visit. In a Canadian trial of 362 women aged 60 and older with stress or mixed incontinence, everyone first had an individual session to learn the contraction. They then did 12 weeks of training either individually or in a group of eight [[7]](#source-7). A year later, leaks had dropped by a median of 70% with individual training and 74% with group training, so the group format was just as good [[7]](#source-7). The 2024 Cochrane review reached the same conclusion: individual or group supervision probably makes little to no difference [[6]](#source-6).

## During and after pregnancy

Pregnancy and childbirth are major risk factors, and about one-third of women have urinary incontinence after giving birth [[8]](#source-8). A Cochrane review of 46 trials with 10,832 women found that continent women who did structured pelvic floor training during pregnancy were 62% less likely to report leaking in late pregnancy, and somewhat less likely 3 to 6 months after birth [[8]](#source-8).

The evidence for treating leaking that has already started during or after pregnancy was less certain [[8]](#source-8). The authors noted that this contrasts with the stronger evidence in women in midlife, and that more research is needed [[8]](#source-8).

## When urgency is part of the problem

If you leak on the way to the bathroom, bladder training may be part of your plan. It teaches you to gradually stretch the time between feeling the urge and going [[3]](#source-3). The NHS says a course usually lasts at least 6 weeks [[3]](#source-3). A Cochrane review found that the evidence for bladder training was limited and came from small trials of variable quality, but it suggests the approach may help [[9]](#source-9). For mixed incontinence, guidelines pair bladder training with pelvic floor training [[4]](#source-4).

## Weight loss helps

Obesity is a known risk factor for leaking [[1]](#source-1), and losing weight can help. In a US trial of 338 overweight and obese women who leaked at least 10 times a week, a 6-month program of diet, exercise and behavior change led to an average weight loss of 8%. Leaks dropped by 47% in that group, compared with 28% in women who received education only [[10]](#source-10). The weight loss group had a bigger drop in stress leaks in particular [[10]](#source-10).

## What a physical therapist adds

The programs that worked in trials were structured and lasted months. A physical therapist who works with the pelvic floor can:

- Check whether you are squeezing the right muscles and how strong and coordinated they are
- Build a program with enough contractions, done often enough and for long enough [[3]](#source-3)
- Add bladder training if urgency is part of the picture [[4]](#source-4)
- Fit the exercises into your daily routine, including the activities that make you leak
- Keep you on track, since the trials that worked asked women to train for months, not days [[3]](#source-3) [[5]](#source-5)

If symptoms do not improve after a full program, your doctor can talk with you about other options.

## The takeaway

- Leaking when you cough, laugh or jump is stress incontinence. It is common, and it is not an inevitable part of aging.
- Guidelines recommend pelvic floor muscle training as the first treatment for stress incontinence, based on high-quality evidence.
- In trials, women who trained were about eight times more likely to report a cure than women who did not.
- Plan on at least 8 contractions, at least 3 times a day, for at least 3 months, ideally after a specialist checks that you are doing them correctly.
- Bladder training helps with urgency, and weight loss reduces leaks in women who are overweight.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If you leak when you cough, sneeze, laugh or exercise, an evaluation can check how your pelvic floor muscles are working and build a training plan you can do at home and keep up long enough to see results.
