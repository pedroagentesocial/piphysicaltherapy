Months after having a baby, your belly still pokes out above or below the belly button. When you sit up from lying down, a ridge rises down the middle of your stomach, or you can press your fingers into a soft gap. Social media tells you to never do a crunch again and that only a special program, or surgery, will close it.

That gap is called diastasis recti, and it is one of the most common changes after pregnancy. The research is more reassuring than the internet: in many women the gap shrinks on its own during the first year, exercise is safe, and the fear around crunches is not supported by the best trial so far [[2]](#source-2) [[6]](#source-6). Here is what the evidence shows.

## What diastasis recti is

The rectus abdominis, the "six-pack" muscle, runs down the front of the belly in two halves. A band of tissue called the linea alba joins them in the middle [[1]](#source-1). As the uterus grows during pregnancy, that band thins and stretches, and the two halves move apart [[1]](#source-1). After delivery the band can recover, but if it has lost some of its elasticity the gap does not close as much as it should [[1]](#source-1).

A separation of more than 2 cm is the definition used by the European Hernia Society [[9]](#source-9). Clinicians often describe it in finger widths, and a gap of two or more fingers is a common threshold [[1]](#source-1).

## How common it is

Very common. Two cohort studies followed first-time mothers:

- In Portugal, ultrasound found diastasis in 100% of women at 35 weeks of pregnancy. By 6 months after birth it had dropped to 39% [[3]](#source-3).
- In Norway, 300 first-time mothers were examined by hand from pregnancy to a year after birth. Diastasis was found in 33.1% at 21 weeks of pregnancy, 60.0% at 6 weeks after birth, 45.4% at 6 months and 32.6% at 12 months [[2]](#source-2).

So the share of women with a gap fell by almost half between 6 weeks and 1 year after birth. In the Norwegian study, women who reported heavy lifting 20 or more times a week were about twice as likely to have diastasis. Otherwise, the researchers found no difference in risk factors between women with and without it [[2]](#source-2). In the Portuguese study, weight before pregnancy, weight gain, the baby's birth weight and belly size were not different between women who still had a gap at 6 months and those who did not [[3]](#source-3). Cleveland Clinic lists other factors, such as having twins, being over 35 and having pregnancies close together [[1]](#source-1).

## What it does and does not cause

A common fear is that the gap leads to back pain, leaking or prolapse. The studies are mostly reassuring:

- **Back and pelvic pain:** women with and without diastasis reported the same amount of low back and pelvic pain 12 months after birth [[2]](#source-2), and at 6 months in the Portuguese study [[3]](#source-3).
- **Pelvic floor problems:** a Norwegian study of 72 matched women found no higher rate of leaking, prolapse or other pelvic floor disorders in women with diastasis [[4]](#source-4).
- **Strength and belly pain:** the same study found that women with diastasis tended to have weaker abdominal muscles and reported more abdominal pain [[4]](#source-4).

Cleveland Clinic notes that the separation itself does not hurt, but people may notice a bulge, a soft feeling around the belly button, "doming" when they tense their abs and a sense of a weak core when lifting [[1]](#source-1). For many women, how the belly looks is the main worry, and the European guideline names body image and core instability as the most relevant symptoms [[9]](#source-9).

## Do crunches make it worse?

This is the question most women ask, and the answer has changed.

Many sources, including Cleveland Clinic, still advise avoiding crunches and sit-ups [[1]](#source-1). But researchers in Norway measured the gap with ultrasound while women with diastasis did different exercises. Head lifts and curl-ups actually *narrowed* the gap while they were being done. Drawing the belly in hard and squeezing the pelvic floor *widened* it below the belly button [[5]](#source-5).

The same team then ran a trial in 70 women 6 to 12 months after birth. Half did a 12-week home program of head lifts, curl-ups and twisted curl-ups 5 days a week. The other half did nothing [[6]](#source-6). The results:

| Outcome after 12 weeks | Curl-up program vs. no exercise |
| --- | --- |
| Width of the gap | No change, neither better nor worse [[6]](#source-6) |
| Abdominal muscle thickness | Increased [[6]](#source-6) |
| Abdominal strength | Increased [[6]](#source-6) |
| Pelvic floor symptoms and back, pelvic or belly pain | No meaningful change [[6]](#source-6) |

In other words, curl-ups did not close the gap, but they did not widen it either, and they made the muscles stronger [[6]](#source-6).

## Can exercise close the gap?

Not by much, according to two systematic reviews:

- A 2021 review of 7 trials with 381 women found only very low quality evidence for any specific program. Deep abdominal (transversus) training narrowed the gap by about 0.6 cm in two small trials, and pelvic floor training alone did not narrow it [[7]](#source-7).
- A 2023 review of 16 trials with 698 women after birth found that abdominal exercise narrowed the gap by about 0.4 cm compared with usual care. The authors judged that too small to matter clinically. They added that abdominal exercise may still have other physical and emotional benefits [[8]](#source-8).

Some small trials report larger effects, but their quality was low. Only 3 of the 16 trials in the 2023 review had a low risk of bias [[8]](#source-8).

That does not mean exercise is pointless. The goal shifts from "closing the gap" to building a strong, capable trunk for lifting a baby, a car seat and groceries. That is something the trial showed exercise can do [[6]](#source-6).

## Practical steps

- **Give it time.** Many gaps shrink over the first year without any special treatment [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3).
- **Early on, be gentle.** For the first 6 weeks, Cleveland Clinic suggests not lifting anything heavier than your baby and rolling onto your side to get out of bed [[1]](#source-1).
- **Then build strength gradually.** Abdominal exercises, including curl-ups, are safe for the gap and build strength [[6]](#source-6). If you see doming, a physical therapist can help you adjust the exercise and the load.
- **Do not count on a binder.** A belly band can feel supportive, but it does not heal the gap or strengthen your muscles [[1]](#source-1).
- **Get checked if you have pain or a bulge at the belly button.** Diastasis can occur with an umbilical hernia, which is treated differently [[1]](#source-1) [[9]](#source-9).

## When surgery comes up

Surgery to repair diastasis is usually done by sewing the two halves of the muscle back together (plication), sometimes as part of a tummy tuck [[1]](#source-1) [[9]](#source-9). The guidelines are cautious:

- The European Hernia Society suggests physical therapy may be considered before surgery. It suggests plication when there is no hernia, and a repair with mesh when there is a midline hernia. Most of its recommendations are weak because the research is limited [[9]](#source-9).
- Swedish national recommendations call physical therapy the first treatment. They advise considering surgery only for people with functional problems, and only after a standardized 6-month core training program. They also advise against surgery if another pregnancy is planned [[10]](#source-10).

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can:

- Measure the gap and check for doming and for a hernia that needs a doctor's review [[1]](#source-1) [[9]](#source-9)
- Separate the gap from other causes of back, pelvic or pelvic floor symptoms, since the gap itself is often not the cause [[2]](#source-2) [[4]](#source-4)
- Build a progressive abdominal program that includes curl-ups and other trunk exercises at a pace that fits your recovery [[6]](#source-6)
- Teach you how to lift your baby, car seat and other loads while your strength comes back [[1]](#source-1)
- Run the structured training period recommended before surgery is considered [[10]](#source-10)

## The takeaway

- Diastasis recti is very common. About 6 in 10 first-time mothers have it at 6 weeks after birth, and about 1 in 3 at a year.
- In studies, it was not linked to more back pain, pelvic pain, leaking or prolapse, though women with it tended to have weaker abs and more belly pain.
- Curl-ups did not widen the gap in a randomized trial and made the abdominal muscles stronger.
- Exercise narrows the gap only slightly, so aim for strength and function rather than a number.
- Surgery is an option for some people with ongoing problems, usually after about 6 months of structured training and when no more pregnancies are planned.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If your belly still feels weak or bulges months after giving birth, an evaluation can measure the gap, rule out a hernia and build a plan to get your core strong again.
