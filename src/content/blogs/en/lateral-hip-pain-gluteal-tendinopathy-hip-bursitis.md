Pain on the outside of the hip, right over the bony point you can feel at the side, is often labeled hip bursitis. The pain can spread down the outside of the thigh, and it tends to be worse at night when lying on that side, when getting up from a chair, and with long walks, stairs or squatting [[1]](#source-1).

Clinicians now call this greater trochanteric pain syndrome, and in most people the main source is not the bursa but the tendons of the gluteal muscles, which attach to that bony point [[2]](#source-2). The difference matters, because it changes what helps.

## Usually tendons, not bursitis

In an ultrasound study of 877 people with this kind of hip pain, 80% had no bursitis at all. About half had changes in the gluteal tendons, and 28% had a thickened iliotibial band, the band of tissue running down the outside of the thigh [[3]](#source-3). The authors concluded that the pain usually comes from a combination of the gluteal tendons and the iliotibial band, with bursitis present only in a minority [[3]](#source-3).

## Who gets it

It is common, especially in women. In a study of 3,026 adults aged 50 to 79, it affected one hip in 15% of women and both hips in 8.5%, compared with 6.6% and 1.9% of men [[4]](#source-4). It is particularly common after menopause [[2]](#source-2).

People with low back pain, knee arthritis or tenderness along the iliotibial band were more likely to have it. Body weight was not linked once those factors were taken into account [[4]](#source-4). People who had it in both hips walked and stood up from a chair more slowly [[4]](#source-4).

## How it is diagnosed

The diagnosis is mostly made with an exam. In a study of 65 people with pain on the outside of the hip, two simple findings were useful [[5]](#source-5):

- **Pain within 30 seconds of standing on the painful leg** made gluteal tendon changes on MRI very likely.
- **No pain when the bony point was pressed** made them unlikely.

The study was small, so these tests support a clinical exam rather than replace it [[5]](#source-5). Because spine problems are a known risk factor [[1]](#source-1) and low back pain often goes along with it [[4]](#source-4), a good exam usually checks your lower back too.

## How long does it last?

It can linger. In a study of 164 people seen by their family doctor for this problem, at least 36% still had pain after 1 year and 29% after 5 years. People who also had arthritis in the legs were nearly 5 times as likely to still have pain at 1 year [[6]](#source-6).

## What the trials found

The largest trial, from Australia, included 204 people aged 35 to 70 who had pain for more than 3 months and tendon changes confirmed on MRI. They were randomly assigned to one of three approaches [[7]](#source-7):

- **Education plus exercise:** 14 sessions with a physical therapist over 8 weeks, plus daily home exercises
- **One steroid injection**
- **Wait and see**, with no treatment

Education plus exercise did better than the injection at 8 weeks and at 1 year on how people rated their hip overall. At 1 year, pain levels were similar after exercise and after the injection, and both were better than waiting [[7]](#source-7). No serious side effects were reported in any group [[7]](#source-7).

The education part taught people to avoid positions and movements that press the gluteal tendons against the bone, and to build up the load on the tendon gradually. The exercises progressively strengthened the hip muscles that move the leg out to the side and that control the hip during daily movements [[7]](#source-7).

## What about a steroid shot?

A single steroid injection into the area can give relief that lasts a few months, but the number of injections should be limited, because repeated shots may damage nearby tissue [[1]](#source-1). Trials show the pattern clearly:

| Trial | Short term | Longer term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brinks, 2011 [[8]](#source-8): 120 people in primary care | At 3 months, 55% recovered after an injection vs. 34% with usual care | At 12 months, 61% vs. 60%: no difference |
| Rompe, 2009 [[9]](#source-9): 229 people with long-lasting pain | At 1 month, the injection did best (75% success vs. 7% with home training) | At 15 months, the injection did worst (48% vs. 80% with home training) |

In other words, a shot can help you through a bad patch, but it does not seem to change the longer-term course the way a good exercise program can.

## What you can do now

- **Change how you sit, stand and lie down.** Positions that pull the thigh across the body squeeze the tendons against the bone. Common ones are crossing your legs or knees, sitting with your knees pressed together, standing with your weight hanging on one hip, and lying on your side, which compresses the tendons on both hips [[2]](#source-2). If you do sleep on your side, pillows between your knees and shins can help [[2]](#source-2).
- **Avoid what clearly makes it worse for now**, such as long walks or many stairs, while you build up strength [[1]](#source-1).
- **Strengthen, step by step.** Specific hip strengthening was the core of the trial program that worked best [[7]](#source-7).
- **Skip stretches that pull the leg across your body.** Stretches often given for the iliotibial band both squeeze and pull on the gluteal tendons, and tendon specialists advise avoiding them [[2]](#source-2).
- **Use anti-inflammatory medicine with care** and only for short periods [[1]](#source-1).

Surgery is rarely needed [[1]](#source-1).

## When to get medical help

**Call 911 or go to the emergency room** if you have severe hip pain after a fall or injury, cannot walk or put weight on your leg, or have tingling or loss of feeling in your hip or leg after an injury [[10]](#source-10).

**Get urgent medical advice** for severe hip pain that started suddenly without a fall or injury [[10]](#source-10).

**See a clinician** if hip pain is stopping you from doing normal activities or affecting your sleep, keeps getting worse or coming back, or has not improved after 2 weeks of self-care [[10]](#source-10).

## The takeaway

- Pain on the outside of the hip is usually a gluteal tendon problem. True bursitis is the exception.
- It is common in women over 50 and can last for months or years without the right approach.
- In the largest trial, education plus exercise beat a steroid shot and waiting, and the benefit lasted at least a year.
- A shot can calm the pain for a few weeks or months, but its benefit fades.
- Avoiding positions that squeeze the tendons, such as crossing your legs or lying on that side, is part of the treatment.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If the outside of your hip hurts when you lie on it or climb stairs, an evaluation can confirm where the pain is coming from and build a strengthening program that loads the tendons at the right pace.
