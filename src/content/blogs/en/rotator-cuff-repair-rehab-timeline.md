Rotator cuff repair reattaches a torn shoulder tendon to the bone. The repair needs months to heal and get strong, and rehab is what gets your shoulder moving and working again. Here is what the research and the surgeons’ guidelines say about the road back.

## The usual timeline

Every surgeon has their own protocol, and the pace depends on the size of the tear, the quality of the tissue and the type of repair [[2]](#source-2) [[5]](#source-5). A typical path looks like this:

| Stage | What usually happens |
| --- | --- |
| First 4 to 6 weeks | Sling most of the time, with gentle passive motion: a therapist or your other arm moves the shoulder for you [[2]](#source-2) |
| From about 4 to 6 weeks | Active exercises, moving the arm with your own muscles [[2]](#source-2) |
| From about 8 to 12 weeks | Strengthening starts [[2]](#source-2) |
| About 4 to 6 months | Most people have useful motion and enough strength for daily life [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3) |
| Return to your previous work | Varies widely; about 8 months on average in one review, and harder for physically demanding jobs [[16]](#source-16) |

*Sources: AAOS OrthoInfo [[2]](#source-2); MedlinePlus [[3]](#source-3); Haunschild et al. [[16]](#source-16).*

The American Society of Shoulder and Elbow Therapists describes a similar framework: a short period of strict protection, protected passive motion through about week 6, then active motion, with progressive strengthening from about week 12 [[5]](#source-5). Expect several months before you are back to your regular activities [[3]](#source-3).

## Early motion or a longer stay in the sling?

This is one of the most studied questions in shoulder rehab. The short answer: for many people, starting to move earlier does not seem to harm the repair, but it does not make a big long-term difference either.

- **Moving early.** The 2025 AAOS guideline gives a strong recommendation: for small to medium tears, outcomes are similar whether motion starts early (0 to 2 weeks) or later (4 to 8 weeks) [[1]](#source-1). A review of 20 trials found early rehab gave a few more degrees of motion in the first months, but pain and function were the same, and the difference in motion had mostly faded by a year [[6]](#source-6).
- **Coming out of the sling sooner.** The guideline also says that, for certain patients, weaning off the sling right away to use the arm for daily activities does not harm outcomes, and that the timing should be a shared decision with your surgeon [[1]](#source-1). Early active movement improved motion at 6 weeks without changing tendon healing [[7]](#source-7).
- **The caution.** Large tears are less well studied. An earlier analysis found early motion was linked to more re-tears once trials limited to small and medium tears were set aside [[8]](#source-8). That is one reason your surgeon may keep you protected longer after a big repair.

Here is what the trials comparing earlier movement with standard sling use found on imaging:

| Trial | People | Early approach | Standard approach | Re-tear or healing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sheps, 2019 [[9]](#source-9) | 206 | Weaned off the sling, early active motion | Sling for 6 weeks | Full-thickness tear at 12 months: 30% vs. 33%, no significant difference |
| Keener, 2014 [[12]](#source-12) | 124, under 65 | Early passive motion | 6 weeks of immobilization | 92% healed overall, no difference between groups |
| Tirefort, 2019 [[10]](#source-10) | 80 | No sling | Sling | No difference in tendon integrity at 6 months |
| Littlewood, 2021 [[11]](#source-11) | 73 | Early patient-directed rehab | Sling for 4 weeks | Re-tear at 12 weeks: 7 of 23 vs. 11 of 27, in a small pilot trial |

A large UK trial of early, patient-directed rehab versus standard sling use is under way, with results still awaited [[13]](#source-13). Until then, follow the plan your surgeon and therapist set for your repair.

## How often do repairs re-tear?

More often than many people expect, and it is not always a disaster. A review of more than 8,000 shoulders found an average re-tear rate of 26.6% about 2 years after surgery [[14]](#source-14). Re-tears were more likely with larger tears, more fatty change in the muscle and older age [[14]](#source-14), and the odds rose about 5% for each year of age [[15]](#source-15).

The reassuring part: people’s symptoms generally improved whether or not the repair stayed intact [[14]](#source-14). Orthopedic surgeons note that people who re-tear usually do not have more pain or less function, and repeat surgery is needed only for severe pain or loss of function [[2]](#source-2).

Factors that can lower the chance of a good result include poor tissue quality, large or massive tears, not taking part fully in rehab, age over 65 and smoking or other nicotine use [[2]](#source-2) [[3]](#source-3). Some of these you cannot change, but rehab and avoiding nicotine are in your hands.

## Driving, work and sleep

- **Driving:** plan on not driving for a few weeks, and only once your surgeon or therapist clears you. Never drive while taking opioid pain medicine [[4]](#source-4).

- **Work:** a review of 13 studies found that 62.3% of people returned to their previous level of work, on average about 8 months after surgery, and the return was harder for more physically demanding jobs [[16]](#source-16).
- **Pain:** the AAOS guideline supports combining different pain treatments or using non-opioid options to limit opioids [[1]](#source-1). Stop opioids as soon as your pain improves, and talk to your doctor if pain does not get better within a few weeks [[2]](#source-2).
- **Ice:** use ice wrapped in a towel 4 to 6 times a day for about 20 minutes to help with swelling [[4]](#source-4).

## When to call your surgeon

Call your surgeon or nurse if you have [[4]](#source-4):

- Bleeding that soaks through the dressing and does not stop with pressure
- Pain that your medicine does not relieve
- Swelling in the arm
- Numbness or tingling in the fingers or hand, or fingers that look darker or feel cool
- Redness, pain, swelling or yellow drainage from the wound
- A temperature above 101°F (38.3°C)

**Call 911** if you have pain and swelling in a leg or arm together with shortness of breath or chest pain, which can be signs of a blood clot [[18]](#source-18).

## The takeaway

- Full recovery from rotator cuff repair takes months: most people have useful strength by 4 to 6 months, and heavy work can take longer.
- For small to medium tears, moving early and leaving the sling sooner appear safe, but larger repairs may need more protection.
- Re-tears are fairly common, yet most people still end up with less pain and better function.
- Rehab, patience and avoiding nicotine are the parts you control.
- Fever, wound drainage, numb or cold fingers, or leg swelling with breathing trouble need prompt attention.
