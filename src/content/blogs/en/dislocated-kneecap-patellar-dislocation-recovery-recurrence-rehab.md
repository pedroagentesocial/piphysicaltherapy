A teenager plants a foot and turns to cut on the basketball court, and the knee gives way. The kneecap has slid out to the side of the knee. Sometimes it pops back in on its own within seconds. Sometimes it stays out until a doctor moves it back.

Either way, it is frightening, and the questions that follow are the same: how long until the knee feels normal, and will it happen again? Here is what the research says.

## What happens in a dislocation

The kneecap normally glides up and down in a groove at the end of the thighbone. Ligaments, muscles and the shape of the bones keep it in that groove. When the kneecap slips completely out of it, that is a dislocation [[1]](#source-1).

It often happens without any contact, during a pivot, a twist or an awkward fall. A direct blow to the kneecap can also knock it out of place [[1]](#source-1).

A dislocated kneecap often moves back into place by itself. Even then, it still needs to be checked by a doctor, usually with X-rays [[2]](#source-2). An MRI may be used to look for loose pieces of bone or cartilage [[1]](#source-1), because a dislocation can damage the underside of the kneecap and the end of the thighbone [[1]](#source-1).

## Who it happens to

Teenagers are the main group. In a 21-year US population study of 609 people, first-time dislocations happened at a rate of 23.2 per 100,000 people a year overall, but 147.7 per 100,000 among those aged 14 to 18. The average age was 21, and men and women were affected at similar rates [[3]](#source-3). An earlier study of 189 people found the highest risk in girls aged 10 to 17 [[4]](#source-4).

## Will it dislocate again?

This is the main concern, and the answer depends a lot on the person. A first dislocation does not always lead to more. In the earlier study, 17% of people with a first-time dislocation had another episode of instability over 2 to 5 years, compared with 49% of those who had already had problems with that kneecap [[4]](#source-4).

Over the long run, the numbers climb. In a group of 584 people followed for an average of 12 years after a first dislocation, about 36% had dislocated the same knee again by 20 years [[5]](#source-5). Children and teens who were still growing at the time of the first dislocation had the highest risk:

Much of the risk comes from the shape of the knee itself, which is why an exam and imaging matter. In the study of 584 people, these features were linked with another dislocation [[5]](#source-5):

| Feature | What it means | Link with another dislocation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shallow groove (trochlear dysplasia) | The groove the kneecap rides in is too flat | About 18 times the odds |
| High-riding kneecap (patella alta) | The kneecap sits higher than usual | About 10 times the odds |
| First dislocation before 18 | Still young at the first injury | About 2.4 times the odds |
| Tendon attached farther to the outside (high TT-TG distance) | The pull on the kneecap is angled outward | About 2 times the odds |
| Sex | Being a girl or woman | About 1.5 times the odds |

Shallow grooves, a high-riding kneecap and the angle of the tendon pull also appear in OrthoInfo's list of reasons a kneecap is more likely to move out of place, along with being knock-kneed [[1]](#source-1). These features also predicted that the next dislocation would come sooner [[5]](#source-5).

## What recovery looks like

Many people can start walking again soon after treatment, and a crutch can help if walking hurts. A knee support may be used, and your doctor or physiotherapist will show you exercises to do regularly [[2]](#source-2). Full recovery can take several months [[2]](#source-2).

At home, the NHS suggests [[2]](#source-2):

- Holding an ice pack wrapped in a cloth on the knee for about 10 to 15 minutes, at least 3 times a day.
- Keeping the leg raised and supported when resting to reduce swelling.
- Doing your knee exercises through the day so the knee does not get stiff, and staying as active as you can.
- Avoiding twisting the knee, and not playing sport until it has fully recovered its usual strength.

## How physical therapy helps

For a first-time dislocation, doctors most often recommend treatment without surgery: exercises and bracing, with a gradual return to sport [[1]](#source-1). The exercises strengthen the thigh muscles to help keep the kneecap on track, and cycling is often part of the program. The goal is to return to normal activities within 1 to 3 months [[1]](#source-1).

A typical plan moves through these steps:

- **Calm the knee** in the first days with ice, elevation and support [[2]](#source-2).
- **Restore motion** so the knee does not stiffen [[2]](#source-2).
- **Strengthen the thigh and hip** to help control the kneecap [[1]](#source-1).
- **Return to sport gradually**, once strength has recovered [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2).

## When surgery is considered

Surgery may be needed if the knee was badly damaged, for example by a loose piece of cartilage, or if the kneecap has dislocated more than once [[2]](#source-2). It is usually reserved for people with repeated instability, a kneecap that tracks badly as the knee moves, or a first dislocation with very high risk [[1]](#source-1). Procedures can tighten the ligaments that hold the kneecap, correct the alignment of the bones or reshape the groove. OrthoInfo reports that fewer than 10% dislocate again after these operations [[1]](#source-1).

Whether to operate after a first dislocation is less clear. A Cochrane review of 10 trials with 519 participants found that surgery may lower the chance of another dislocation over 2 to 9 years: in its example, about 35 of 100 people treated without surgery dislocated again, compared with about 19 of 100 after surgery [[8]](#source-8). But the certainty of the evidence was very low, and it was uncertain whether surgery improved knee function or caused more complications [[8]](#source-8).

A Swedish trial in children shows why the question is hard. Ten years after a first dislocation, the kneecap had dislocated again in 80% of the children treated with a brace and in 62% of those who had ligament repair surgery, a difference that was not statistically significant, and knee function was similar [[9]](#source-9). The authors did not support routine surgery for every child, but stressed that children treated without surgery need structured follow-up [[9]](#source-9).

## The long view: protecting the joint

A dislocation can harm the cartilage, and that matters years later. In that same population of 609 people, about 15% had arthritis behind the kneecap 20 years after a first dislocation, compared with about 3% of people of the same age and sex who never dislocated. The risk was higher in people with cartilage damage from the injury, repeated dislocations or a shallow groove [[7]](#source-7). The need for a knee replacement, however, was similar in both groups [[7]](#source-7).

That is one more reason to take a first dislocation seriously, get the knee checked and finish the rehab.

## When to get medical help

**Go to the emergency room or call 911** if your knee has changed shape, becomes very swollen very quickly, you felt a pop or snap, or you cannot move your leg because of the pain. Do not drive yourself [[2]](#source-2).

**Get medical advice the same day** if your knee is very painful, swollen or bruised, you cannot stand or move it, or you think the kneecap dislocated and moved back into place [[2]](#source-2).

**While you wait**, keep the knee still and supported, and do not try to put the kneecap back in place yourself [[2]](#source-2).

## The takeaway

- Kneecap dislocations are most common in teenagers, and the kneecap often slips back on its own. It still needs to be checked.
- After a first dislocation, many people never have another one, but the risk is higher in young people still growing and in knees with a shallow groove or a high-riding kneecap.
- Most first-time dislocations are treated with exercise, sometimes a brace and a gradual return to sport.
- Surgery may lower the chance of another dislocation, but the evidence is very uncertain. It is usually considered for repeated dislocations, cartilage damage or very high-risk knees.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. After a dislocated kneecap, an evaluation can check your strength and the way your knee moves, and guide you or your teen back to sport at a safe pace.
