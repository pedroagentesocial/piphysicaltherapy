A sharp pain deep in the groin when you twist, squat or pivot. A hip that feels stiff and pinches when you pull your knee toward your chest. For many active teens and adults, that is how hip impingement shows up, and an MRI may add a second label: a labral tear.

Femoroacetabular impingement, or FAI, means the ball and socket of the hip are shaped in a way that lets them rub against each other during certain movements. Over time, that rubbing can damage the labrum, the rim of cartilage around the socket, and the cartilage lining the joint [[1]](#source-1). Both physical therapy and hip arthroscopy are used to treat it, and several trials have now compared them head to head.

## Two shapes of hip

There are two main types of impingement, and many people have some of both [[1]](#source-1):

- **Cam:** the ball at the top of the thigh bone is not perfectly round, so it cannot rotate smoothly inside the socket.
- **Pincer:** extra bone extends over the normal rim of the socket.

These shapes form while the skeleton is growing. Athletes may notice symptoms earlier, but exercise does not cause FAI [[1]](#source-1). Pain, stiffness and limping are common. Pain is often felt in the groin, and turning, twisting and squatting may bring on a sharp, stabbing pain [[1]](#source-1).

## The scan is not the whole story

Bone shape alone is not a diagnosis. An international consensus of hip specialists, the Warwick Agreement, introduced the term "FAI syndrome" to make clear that symptoms are central: the diagnosis needs the right symptoms, positive findings on a clinical exam and matching imaging [[2]](#source-2).

The reason is that these findings are common in people with no pain at all:

| Finding in people without hip pain | How often |
| --- | --- |
| Cam shape, all studies combined [[3]](#source-3) | 37% |
| Cam shape in athletes [[3]](#source-3) | 54.8% |
| Cam shape in the general population [[3]](#source-3) | 23.1% |
| Labral tear on MRI, one blinded study [[4]](#source-4) | 69% |

In that blinded study, 45 volunteers with no history of hip pain or injury had an MRI, and 73% of their hips showed at least one abnormality [[4]](#source-4). A review of 26 studies concluded that FAI shapes and labral injuries are common in people without symptoms, so decisions should rest on the history and exam as much as on the images [[3]](#source-3).

So if an MRI shows a labral tear, it may or may not be the cause of your pain. That is worth knowing before deciding on surgery.

## What the trials found

Three randomized trials have compared hip arthroscopy with physical therapy for FAI syndrome, and a fourth, small trial compared surgery with a sham (placebo) operation.

**UK FASHIoN** was the largest. It included 348 people aged 16 and older at 23 hospitals in the UK. Half had arthroscopy, and half had personalized hip therapy: an individualized, supervised and progressive program led by a physical therapist [[5]](#source-5). The program called for at least 6 sessions, and participants attended an average of 6 sessions of about 30 minutes [[5]](#source-5).

Both groups improved. After adjusting for differences between them, arthroscopy came out 6.8 points ahead at 12 months, just above the 6.1-point difference considered meaningful to patients [[5]](#source-5). Muscle soreness was the most common side effect in both groups. Five of the 6 serious adverse events in the surgery group were related to treatment, and one person developed a hip joint infection after surgery [[5]](#source-5).

The other trials:

| Trial | Who took part | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FAIT, 2019 [[6]](#source-6) | 222 adults aged 18 to 60 in England | At 8 months, daily-activity scores were 10 points higher after surgery than after up to 8 physical therapy sessions over 5 months |
| Mansell, 2018 [[7]](#source-7) | 80 patients at a U.S. military hospital | No significant difference at 2 years; 28 people assigned to physical therapy had surgery anyway |
| HIPARTI, 2026 [[9]](#source-9) | 30 patients, surgery vs. sham surgery | No significant difference at 1 or 2 years; both groups improved |

A 2021 review that pooled the three trials comparing surgery with nonsurgical care found high-certainty evidence that surgery gave better hip-related quality of life at 12 months, by about 11 points. At 24 months, the evidence was low quality and suggested the results were similar [[8]](#source-8). The authors called for placebo-controlled trials to show how much of the benefit comes from the surgery itself [[8]](#source-8).

HIPARTI is the first of those. In 30 patients who all did 3 months of physical therapy afterward, real arthroscopy was not better than sham surgery on the main measure at any time point. The surgery group did show more consistent gains on some secondary measures, and the authors note the trial was too small to detect a difference, so a larger study is needed [[9]](#source-9).

## Putting it together

The evidence is genuinely mixed, and it is worth being honest about it:

- **Surgery tends to win in the first year** in the larger trials, by a modest margin [[5]](#source-5) [[6]](#source-6) [[8]](#source-8).
- **The gap is less certain by 2 years** [[7]](#source-7) [[8]](#source-8), and a small placebo trial did not show a clear benefit of surgery itself [[9]](#source-9).
- **Physical therapy helped many people** in every trial, without the risks of an operation [[5]](#source-5) [[7]](#source-7).
- **Labral tears and cam shapes are common without pain** [[3]](#source-3) [[4]](#source-4), so a scan finding alone is not a reason for surgery.

For many people, a structured physical therapy program is a reasonable first step, with arthroscopy as an option if symptoms persist. The Warwick Agreement lists conservative care, rehabilitation and surgery all as suitable treatments [[2]](#source-2), and the choice depends on how much the hip limits you, your goals and how you respond to therapy.

## What hip therapy usually includes

OrthoInfo lists changing or avoiding the activities that cause symptoms, anti-inflammatory medicine and physical therapy as nonsurgical options. Exercises aim to improve hip range of motion and strengthen the muscles that support the joint [[1]](#source-1). In the trials, programs were individualized and progressive [[5]](#source-5), and the FAIT program focused on core stability and movement control [[6]](#source-6).

In practice, that usually means:

- **Finding the positions that provoke pain**, such as deep squats or sitting with the hip bent sharply, and adjusting them for now
- **Strengthening the hip and trunk**, progressing gradually
- **Improving how you move** when you squat, lunge, land and change direction
- **Returning to sport in steps**, guided by symptoms

A numbing injection into the hip is sometimes used as a test: if it brings temporary relief, that supports FAI as the source of the pain [[1]](#source-1).

## When to get medical help

**Call 911 or go to the emergency room** if you have severe hip pain after a fall or injury, cannot walk or put weight on your leg, or have tingling or loss of feeling in your hip or leg after an injury [[10]](#source-10).

**Get urgent medical advice** if you have severe hip pain that started suddenly without a fall or injury, or if your hip is swollen and hot, or you feel unwell with a fever [[10]](#source-10).

**See a clinician** if hip pain is stopping you from doing normal activities or affecting your sleep, keeps getting worse or coming back, or has not improved after 2 weeks of self-care [[10]](#source-10).

## The takeaway

- FAI is a mismatch in the shape of the hip's ball and socket that can cause groin pain, especially with twisting, squatting and deep bending.
- Cam shapes and labral tears are common in people with no pain, so the diagnosis depends on symptoms and an exam, not just a scan.
- In the larger trials, arthroscopy led to modestly better results than physical therapy at 8 to 12 months.
- By 2 years the difference is less certain, and a small placebo-controlled trial did not show a clear benefit from the surgery itself.
- Physical therapy helps many people and is a reasonable first step, with surgery still available if it does not.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If your hip pinches when you squat, sit or pivot, an evaluation can check where the pain is coming from and build a program to get you back to your sport or activity.
