It started as a twinge on the inside of your thigh after a hard soccer game. Now it hurts when you kick, sprint or change direction, and it is stiff the morning after. You rest for a week, it feels better, and then it comes right back the first time you play.

Groin pain is one of the most stubborn problems in sports that involve kicking, sprinting and cutting. The most common type comes from the adductors, the muscles on the inside of the thigh [[2]](#source-2) [[5]](#source-5). The good news is that one of the clearest trials in sports medicine showed that an active exercise program works far better than passive treatment, and later trials showed that a short strengthening program can help prevent it [[6]](#source-6) [[10]](#source-10). Here is what the evidence shows.

## How common it is

In European professional soccer, hip and groin injuries made up 12% to 16% of all injuries each season. More than half kept players out for more than a week, the average absence was 15 days and 15% were repeat injuries [[2]](#source-2). Over 15 seasons, hip and groin injuries made up 14% of all time-loss injuries, and 63% of those involved the adductors [[3]](#source-3). Injury rates fell slightly over that time, but the total days lost did not improve [[3]](#source-3).

Those numbers count only injuries that stop players from training or playing. Many athletes keep playing through pain. A Spanish study followed 407 amateur players for a full season. In an average week, 11.7% had groin problems, but only 1.3% missed time. Counting only time-loss injuries captured just 10% of all groin problems [[4]](#source-4).

## The four types of groin pain

Groin pain used to have many confusing names, such as "sports hernia" or "Gilmore's groin." In 2015, 24 experts from 14 countries agreed on a simpler system known as the Doha agreement [[1]](#source-1). It is based on your history and a physical exam, and it sorts groin pain into three groups: four defined clinical entities, hip-related groin pain and other causes [[1]](#source-1).

| Type | What the exam finds |
| --- | --- |
| Adductor-related | Tenderness of the adductor muscles and pain when you squeeze your legs together against resistance [[1]](#source-1) |
| Iliopsoas-related | Tenderness of the hip flexor (iliopsoas). More likely if resisted hip flexion or stretching the hip flexors hurts [[1]](#source-1) |
| Inguinal-related | Pain and tenderness in the area of the inguinal canal, with no hernia you can feel. More likely if resisted sit-ups, coughing or sneezing make it worse [[1]](#source-1) |
| Pubic-related | Tenderness of the pubic bone where the two sides meet, and the bone right next to it [[1]](#source-1) |

The experts agreed that the hip joint should always be considered as a possible cause. They recommended checking hip range of motion and doing hip tests in every athlete with groin pain [[1]](#source-1).

Adductor-related pain is the most common type. In a specialist clinic in Qatar that assessed 100 athletes with groin pain, 61% had adductor-related pain and only 4% had pubic-related pain. In 44%, more than one cause was found [[5]](#source-5). That is one reason a thorough exam matters: treating only one cause may not be enough.

## Active training beats passive treatment

In a Danish trial published in The Lancet, 68 athletes who had had adductor-related groin pain for a median of 40 weeks were randomly assigned to one of two 8- to 12-week programs [[6]](#source-6):

- **Active training:** exercises to build strength and coordination of the muscles that act on the pelvis, especially the adductors
- **Physical therapy without active training:** passive treatments

Four months after treatment, 23 athletes in the active training group had returned to their sport at the same level without groin pain, compared with 4 in the passive group [[6]](#source-6). That was 79% of the active group [[7]](#source-7).

The benefit lasted. When the researchers tracked down 47 of the original 59 participants 8 to 12 years later, the active training group was still doing better, especially the soccer players [[7]](#source-7).

A 2015 systematic review looked at 72 studies on treating groin pain in athletes. Only 4 were high quality [[8]](#source-8). It found moderate evidence that for adductor-related groin pain:

- Active exercises lead to more success than passive treatments [[8]](#source-8)
- A multimodal program that adds manual therapy can shorten the time back to sport compared with active exercises alone [[8]](#source-8)
- Surgery to partly release the adductor tendon improves success over time [[8]](#source-8)

For inguinal-related groin pain, the review found moderate evidence that surgery led to better results than conservative treatment [[8]](#source-8).

## Prevention: one exercise, a big difference

Low adductor strength appears to raise the risk of adductor injuries [[9]](#source-9). The Copenhagen adduction exercise produces high activity in the adductor muscles. In a trial with 24 under-19 soccer players, 8 weeks of the exercise added to normal training increased eccentric adductor strength (strength while the muscle lengthens under load) by 35.7%. Players stuck with it, completing 91% of the sessions, and muscle soreness stayed low [[9]](#source-9).

A Norwegian trial then tested whether that single exercise could prevent groin problems. Thirty-five semi-professional soccer teams, with 652 players, were randomly assigned [[10]](#source-10). The prevention teams did the Adductor Strengthening Programme, based on the Copenhagen adduction exercise with three levels of difficulty. They did it 3 times a week in the 6 to 8 weeks of preseason and once a week during the 28-week season. The other teams trained as usual [[10]](#source-10).

## What recovery usually looks like

Every case is different, but a rehab plan built on the evidence above usually:

- **Starts with a clear diagnosis.** Your physical therapist sorts out whether the pain is adductor-, hip flexor-, inguinal-, pubic- or hip-related, or a mix [[1]](#source-1) [[5]](#source-5).
- **Builds strength step by step.** Progressive strength and coordination exercises for the adductors and the muscles around the pelvis are the core of treatment [[6]](#source-6) [[8]](#source-8).
- **Takes weeks, not days.** In the Danish trial, the program lasted 8 to 12 weeks [[6]](#source-6).
- **Keeps a maintenance dose.** Once you are back, a short weekly adductor strengthening routine can lower the chance of the pain returning [[10]](#source-10).

Pain that does not improve with a good program, or that points to an inguinal problem or the hip joint, may need a referral to a sports medicine doctor or surgeon [[1]](#source-1) [[8]](#source-8).

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can:

- Examine your groin and hip using the Doha system to find which structures are involved [[1]](#source-1)
- Check for more than one cause, since that is common [[5]](#source-5)
- Build an active program for the adductors and pelvic muscles, and progress it until you are back at full speed [[6]](#source-6) [[8]](#source-8)
- Add manual therapy when it may help you return sooner [[8]](#source-8)
- Teach the Copenhagen adduction exercise and plan how often to do it in preseason and in season [[9]](#source-9) [[10]](#source-10)
- Spot signs that point to the hip joint or an inguinal problem and refer you when needed [[1]](#source-1)

## The takeaway

- Groin pain is common in soccer and similar sports, and the adductors are the most frequent source.
- Many athletes play through it, so it is more common than injury reports suggest.
- A clear exam sorts groin pain into adductor-, hip flexor-, inguinal-, pubic- and hip-related types, and more than one can be present.
- Active strengthening of the adductors and pelvic muscles works far better than passive treatment, and the benefit can last for years.
- A single adductor exercise done through preseason and once a week in season lowered the risk of groin problems by 41%.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If groin pain keeps you from kicking, sprinting or changing direction, an evaluation can find where it comes from and build a strengthening plan to get you back on the field and keep you there.
