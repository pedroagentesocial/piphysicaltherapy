Achilles tendinopathy is pain and stiffness in the thick tendon at the back of your ankle, usually with thickening and pain when you load it [[1]](#source-1). You may also hear it called Achilles tendinitis. There are two main types, and the difference matters for your rehab [[1]](#source-1):

- **Midportion:** the pain is more than 2 cm (about 3/4 inch) above where the tendon attaches to the heel
- **Insertional:** the pain is within 2 cm of that attachment

It is usually diagnosed with a physical exam. When the signs fit, imaging is not needed [[1]](#source-1).

## Who gets it

In a large study of recreational runners, 100 of 1,929 (5.2%) developed it while training for a running event [[2]](#source-2). But it is not only a runner’s problem: in a Dutch primary care study, only 35% of midportion cases had a recorded link to sports [[3]](#source-3).

The best-supported risk factor is **a previous episode** [[4]](#source-4). That is one more reason to finish your rehab rather than stopping when the pain eases.

**One risk many people do not know about:** fluoroquinolone antibiotics, such as ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin, carry an FDA boxed warning for tendinitis and tendon rupture, most often in the Achilles [[5]](#source-5). This appears to be rare, but the risk goes up with oral steroids [[6]](#source-6) and is higher in people over 60 and in those with kidney, heart or lung transplants [[5]](#source-5). Problems can start within hours or days, or as long as several months after finishing the medicine [[5]](#source-5). If you are taking ciprofloxacin and get tendon pain, the NIH advises you to stop taking it, rest and call your doctor right away [[7]](#source-7).

## How long does it take?

Think in months, not weeks. The Dutch guideline recommends calf strengthening for **at least 12 weeks**, and says getting back to full, pain-free loading usually takes at least a few months of active treatment [[1]](#source-1). Most people improve, but how much and how fast varies [[8]](#source-8). Pain can last longer than 3 months even with early treatment [[9]](#source-9).

Doing nothing is the weak option. The guideline expects little or no short-term improvement with wait and see [[1]](#source-1), and a review of 29 trials does not recommend it [[10]](#source-10).

The guideline says 23% to 37% of people may still have symptoms for up to 10 years, while 85% of athletes return to sport [[1]](#source-1). Having some symptoms does not always mean you cannot be active: in the 5-year study with the lowest pain-free rate, tendon scores were still much better than at the start [[13]](#source-13).

## What works: loading the tendon

**Exercise is the first-line treatment.** The 2024 physical therapy guideline gives its strongest grade to tendon loading exercise, with loads as high as tolerated [[8]](#source-8). In a review of 29 trials, exercise scored about 20 points better than wait and see on a 0 to 100 scale at 3 months, although the certainty of the evidence was low [[10]](#source-10).

**There is no single magic exercise.** The guideline says eccentric exercise is not required, because different types of loading work [[8]](#source-8). In a trial of 58 people with midportion pain, heavy slow resistance and eccentric training both worked and the results lasted for a year; people attended more of their heavy slow sessions (92% vs. 78%) [[14]](#source-14).

**Complete rest is not the answer.** The guideline says to keep up activities within your pain tolerance [[8]](#source-8). In a trial of 38 people, those who kept running and jumping with pain monitoring did about as well as those who rested from those activities for 6 weeks [[15]](#source-15). This was a small study, and it does not mean you should push through anything. The Dutch guideline advises temporarily stopping or replacing what provokes the pain, then building back gradually [[1]](#source-1). A therapist can help you find the right balance.

**Insertional pain needs a different plan.** Heel drops off a step should only be done for the noninsertional type [[9]](#source-9), and the Dutch guideline suggests starting exercises on a flat surface [[1]](#source-1). In a 2025 trial of 42 active people with insertional pain, a program that limited how far the ankle bent during exercise, removed calf stretching and added heel lifts beat a standard program, with a 24.4 vs. 12.2 point gain at 12 weeks [[16]](#source-16). It was a small trial, and the authors could not say which part of the program made the difference.

**Heel lifts** may be used temporarily [[8]](#source-8). In a trial of 100 people with midportion pain, they did slightly better than eccentric exercise at 12 weeks, but the difference fell just short of what the authors considered meaningful [[17]](#source-17).

## Injections, shockwave and surgery

When exercise is not enough after about 3 months, the Dutch guideline says add-on treatments can be discussed, along with how uncertain they are [[1]](#source-1). Here is what the better studies found:

| Treatment | What the evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection | No benefit over a sham or saline injection in two trials, one with 240 people [[18]](#source-18) and one with 54 [[19]](#source-19) |
| High-volume injection | No improvement over a sham at 12 months in a trial of 192 adults, with or without a steroid [[20]](#source-20) |
| Steroid injection | Evidence conflicts. One trial of 100 people found better results at 6 months when added to exercise [[21]](#source-21), but the high-volume trial found worse results at 26 weeks when a steroid was added [[20]](#source-20). Orthopedic surgeons advise against injecting into the tendon because of the risk of a tear [[9]](#source-9) [[22]](#source-22) |
| Shockwave therapy | A 2026 review of 9 trials found no clinically meaningful benefit and advised against routine use [[23]](#source-23). The Dutch guideline lists it as a possible add-on after 3 months [[1]](#source-1) |
| Night splints, low-level laser | The physical therapy guideline says not to use them [[8]](#source-8) |
| Manual therapy, dry needling, taping | Supported only at the lowest levels of the guideline, such as expert opinion [[8]](#source-8) |

**Surgery** is reserved for people who have not improved after at least 6 months of active treatment [[1]](#source-1) [[9]](#source-9). Recovery can take up to 12 months of rehab, and up to 20% to 30% of patients have continued pain afterward [[9]](#source-9).

## What you can do now

- Reduce what aggravates the tendon and switch to low-impact activity for a while, such as biking, an elliptical or swimming [[9]](#source-9)
- Put an ice pack in a towel on the sore spot for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours [[24]](#source-24)
- Wear comfortable shoes with a low heel and soft sole, and try soft insoles or heel pads [[24]](#source-24)
- Do not use anti-inflammatory pills for more than a month without talking to your doctor [[9]](#source-9)
- Do not rush back: returning to full activity within days is linked to more recurrences [[1]](#source-1)

See a clinician if your heel pain has not improved after 2 weeks of home care, or sooner if it is intense or getting worse [[24]](#source-24). Let them know if you also have back pain that started before age 45 or psoriasis, since the guideline says insertional pain in those cases may call for a check for inflammatory disease [[1]](#source-1).

**Get urgent care for a possible rupture.** Warning signs include a sudden pop in the back of your calf or heel [[9]](#source-9), severe pain and swelling near the heel, and not being able to walk on the leg or stand on your toes [[22]](#source-22).

## The takeaway

- Achilles pain usually takes months to settle, so plan on at least 12 weeks of exercise.
- Progressive loading is the first-line treatment, and complete rest is not.
- Pain near the heel bone needs a different program than pain higher up the tendon.
- PRP and shockwave have not clearly beaten sham treatment, and the evidence on steroid injections conflicts.
- Tell your doctor about tendon pain if you are taking a fluoroquinolone antibiotic, and take a sudden pop seriously.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If your Achilles has been bothering you for weeks, an evaluation can confirm which type you have and build a loading plan that keeps you moving.
