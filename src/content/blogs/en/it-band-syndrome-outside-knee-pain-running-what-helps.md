The first few miles feel fine. Then, at about the same point on every run, a sharp pain starts on the outside of your knee. It gets worse on downhills, and by the end you are limping. You stop, it calms down, and the next run it comes back right on schedule.

That pattern is typical of iliotibial band syndrome, or IT band syndrome. It is the most common cause of pain on the outside of the knee in runners [[1]](#source-1), and it is also common in cyclists and hikers. The research on the best treatment is still thin, but it has changed how we understand the problem, and it points away from simply stretching or foam rolling the band and toward the hip [[3]](#source-3) [[8]](#source-8). Here is what the evidence shows.

## How common it is

A systematic review estimated that IT band syndrome affects between 5% and 14% of runners [[1]](#source-1). A more recent review put it at about 10% of all running injuries [[6]](#source-6).

In a Canadian sports medicine clinic that recorded 2,002 running injuries over two years, IT band syndrome was the second most common injury, after patellofemoral pain (runner's knee). In men, being younger than 34 was linked with a higher risk [[2]](#source-2).

## What the IT band is, and what goes wrong

The IT band is a thick strip of connective tissue that runs down the outside of the thigh, from the hip to just below the knee. It is part of the fascia lata, the sheath that wraps the thigh muscles [[3]](#source-3).

For years, IT band syndrome was described as a friction problem: the band was thought to rub back and forth over the bony bump on the outside of the knee. Anatomy research challenged that idea. In a study of 15 cadavers, MRI scans of healthy volunteers and two athletes with the condition, the band was anchored to the thigh bone by fibrous strands, and no bursa was found. The band did not roll back and forth over the bone. Instead, it pressed against the bone at about 30 degrees of knee bend, and the MRI changes in the athletes were in a layer of fat and nerve-rich tissue under the band [[3]](#source-3).

That explains why the pain often shows up at the same point in each run and on downhills, and why it is usually not a sign that the band is simply "too tight." It is also why many experts now describe it as a compression problem rather than a friction problem [[3]](#source-3) [[8]](#source-8).

## What raises the risk

The research on causes is limited and sometimes conflicting [[1]](#source-1), but two findings come up often.

**How your hip and knee move.** In a study that recorded the running mechanics of healthy female runners and followed them for two years, 18 developed IT band syndrome. Compared with matched runners who stayed healthy, they had shown more hip adduction (the thigh drifting inward) and more inward rotation of the knee while running [[4]](#source-4). Those motions may increase strain on the band and press it against the outside of the knee [[4]](#source-4).

**Hip strength.** In a group of 24 distance runners with IT band syndrome, hip abductor strength (the muscles on the side of the hip that keep the pelvis level) was lower in the injured leg than in the healthy leg and lower than in 30 uninjured runners [[5]](#source-5).

The review authors caution that it is still not clear how big a role hip weakness plays, since the studies were small and results varied [[1]](#source-1).

## What helps

### Hip strengthening

Hip abductor strengthening is the most common part of treatment in the research [[6]](#source-6). In the same study of 24 runners, a 6-week rehab program focused on the gluteus medius increased hip abductor strength by about 35% in women and 51% in men. After 6 weeks, 22 of the 24 runners were pain-free and back to running, and none reported a recurrence at 6 months [[5]](#source-5). That study had no comparison group, so it cannot prove the program caused the recovery, but the improvement in symptoms tracked the gains in strength [[5]](#source-5).

A 2024 systematic review found 13 studies with 201 runners. Treatment programs, mostly built around hip abductor strengthening and sometimes adding shockwave or manual therapy, reduced pain by 27% to 100% and improved function by 10% to 57% over 2 to 8 weeks [[6]](#source-6). The studies were too different to combine, and most were small [[6]](#source-6).

In a small pilot trial of 24 female runners, hip strengthening, IT band stretching and a conventional exercise program were compared for 8 weeks. There was no clear difference between the groups, although the hip strengthening group consistently improved and never scored worse than the others [[7]](#source-7).

### Running mechanics and training load

Because the way the hip and knee move appears to matter [[4]](#source-4), reviews point to hip and knee coordination and running style as key parts of treatment [[1]](#source-1). Running retraining is promising but has not been well tested yet [[6]](#source-6) [[8]](#source-8). Advice about shoes and running surfaces may also help [[1]](#source-1).

### What about stretching and foam rolling?

Many runners stretch or roll the IT band hard. A 2022 review that looked at the forces acting on the outside of the knee concluded that occasional stretching is unlikely to change the band's length or stiffness [[8]](#source-8). Stretching or rolling may still feel good for some people, but it is not the main fix.

### Injections and surgery

In a small trial of 18 runners whose symptoms had started less than two weeks earlier, a corticosteroid injection reduced pain during running over the next two weeks compared with a numbing injection alone [[9]](#source-9). It is a short-term option for some cases, not a replacement for rehab.

A systematic review of 15 studies found that conservative treatment reduced pain in every study that reported on it, with follow-up of up to about 6 months. Surgery is an option for athletes who do not improve, and return-to-sport rates after surgery ranged from 81% to 100%, though most of those studies were lower quality [[10]](#source-10).

## What recovery usually looks like

Every runner is different, but a plan built on this evidence usually:

- **Lowers the load for a while.** Cutting back distance, downhills and pace so the pain settles, without stopping all activity.
- **Builds hip strength step by step.** Exercises for the side and back of the hip, progressed over several weeks [[5]](#source-5) [[6]](#source-6).
- **Looks at how you run.** Checking for the hip drifting inward and the knee rotating in, and working on control [[1]](#source-1) [[4]](#source-4).
- **Brings running back gradually.** In the trials, programs lasted about 2 to 8 weeks [[6]](#source-6).

Pain that does not improve with a good program, swelling, locking or giving way of the knee are reasons to get checked for other problems inside the joint.

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can:

- Confirm that the pain comes from the IT band area and not from the knee joint, the meniscus or the hip
- Measure hip strength and watch how your hip and knee move when you run, squat and step down [[4]](#source-4) [[5]](#source-5)
- Build a progressive hip strengthening program and move it forward as you improve [[5]](#source-5) [[6]](#source-6)
- Help adjust training load, running form, shoes and surfaces [[1]](#source-1)
- Plan a step-by-step return to running so the pain does not keep coming back
- Refer you to a sports medicine doctor if an injection or other treatment may help [[9]](#source-9) [[10]](#source-10)

## The takeaway

- IT band syndrome is the most common cause of pain on the outside of the knee in runners.
- It appears to be a compression problem at the outside of the knee rather than the band rubbing back and forth.
- Runners who develop it tend to let the hip drift inward and the knee rotate in, and many have weaker hip abductors.
- Hip strengthening is the core of treatment in the research, often with changes to training and running form. Stretching the band alone is unlikely to fix it.
- Most runners improve without surgery. Injections and surgery are options for some cases.
