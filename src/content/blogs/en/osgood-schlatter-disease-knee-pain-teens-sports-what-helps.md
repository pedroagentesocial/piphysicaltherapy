Your 12-year-old comes home from practice limping again. The front of the knee, just below the kneecap, hurts when they jump, run, climb stairs or kneel, and there is a tender bump there that seems to be getting bigger. They feel fine at rest, but every game makes it worse.

That pattern is typical of Osgood-Schlatter disease, the most common cause of knee pain in active kids going through a growth spurt [[5]](#source-5). It is often described as something kids simply grow out of, and for many that is true [[1]](#source-1). But newer research shows that the pain can last much longer than expected for some, and that an active plan may work better than just waiting [[5]](#source-5) [[6]](#source-6). Here is what the evidence shows.

## What it is

The patellar tendon runs from the kneecap down to a bony bump on the front of the shin called the tibial tuberosity. In children and teens, that bump is still growing and has a growth area (an apophysis) that is softer than adult bone.

Osgood-Schlatter disease is an irritation of that growth area. The most accepted explanation is that the thigh muscles pull over and over on the tuberosity through the patellar tendon during running, jumping and kicking [[1]](#source-1). Typical signs include [[1]](#source-1) [[2]](#source-2):

- Pain on the front of the shin, just below the kneecap, right over the bony bump
- A dull ache that gets worse with jumping, running, climbing stairs and kneeling
- Tenderness when the bump is pressed
- A bump that looks larger than on the other side
- Pain when straightening the knee against resistance

It can affect one or both knees. An X-ray may show a small separate piece of bone in the tendon, but the diagnosis is usually made from the history and exam [[1]](#source-1).

## Who gets it

**About 1 in 10 adolescents.** In a study of 956 students aged 12 to 15 in Brazil, 9.8% met strict criteria for Osgood-Schlatter disease: 11.0% of boys and 8.3% of girls [[2]](#source-2). Researchers in Denmark also describe it as affecting about 1 in 10 adolescents [[6]](#source-6).

**Kids who play sports.** In a Finnish survey of 389 students, 21.2% of those who had been active in sports at age 13 had had Osgood-Schlatter disease, compared with 4.5% of those who were not active [[3]](#source-3). In the Brazilian study, regular sports practice and tight front thigh muscles (the rectus femoris) were the factors most linked with it [[2]](#source-2).

**During the growth spurt.** Among young athletes seen at a Finnish sports clinic, pain first appeared at an average age of 13.1 years [[3]](#source-3).

## How long it lasts

In most cases, the problem settles once the growth area closes at the end of growth [[1]](#source-1). That can still be a long time for a young athlete. In the Finnish clinic, athletes had to stop training completely for an average of 3.2 months, and the condition got in the way of full training for an average of 7.3 months [[3]](#source-3).

Not every case fades away completely. In a study of 50 patients (69 knees) followed without treatment, 76% felt they had no limits on activity, but 60% still could not kneel without discomfort [[4]](#source-4). In a Danish study that contacted patients 2 to 6 years after diagnosis, 26 of the 43 who answered (60.5%) still had knee pain from Osgood-Schlatter disease, and those with pain had lower sports function and quality of life [[5]](#source-5). The authors concluded that it may not always be self-limiting [[5]](#source-5).

## What helps

### Managing the load instead of just resting

Rest and avoiding painful activities are often the main advice, but that can mean months away from sport [[6]](#source-6). A Danish study tested a more active approach in 51 adolescents aged 10 to 14 who had had pain for an average of 21 months [[6]](#source-6). The 12-week program included:

- An "activity ladder" that set how much running and jumping was allowed, based on pain
- Progressive strengthening exercises for the knee and hip
- A gradual, step-by-step return to sport

After 12 weeks, 80% reported a successful outcome, rising to 90% at 12 months. Knee extension strength improved by 32% and hip abduction strength by 24%, and jumping performance improved too [[6]](#source-6). Return to sport took time: 16% were back playing at 12 weeks and 69% at 12 months [[6]](#source-6).

That study had no comparison group, so it cannot prove the program caused the improvement. Still, it suggests that a structured plan may be a better option than rest or "wait and see" alone [[6]](#source-6).

### What the wider research shows

The evidence on treatment is limited. A 2021 systematic review found only 13 studies, with just two randomized trials, and the quality was poor to moderate. Some approaches, such as stretching, appeared to help, but no trial has compared specific exercises with a sham or usual care [[7]](#source-7). Since tight front thigh muscles are linked with the condition [[2]](#source-2), stretching often goes along with strengthening.

### Surgery

Surgery is rarely needed. It is mainly an option for adults who still have symptoms after growth has ended and who have not improved with conservative care, usually to remove a loose piece of bone [[1]](#source-1).

## What recovery usually looks like

Every young athlete is different, but a plan built on this evidence usually:

- **Lowers the load for a while.** Cutting back on jumping, sprinting and kneeling until the pain settles, without stopping all activity [[6]](#source-6).
- **Uses pain as a guide.** Moving up the activity ladder when pain stays low, and stepping back when it flares [[6]](#source-6).
- **Builds strength step by step.** Exercises for the thigh and hip, progressed over several weeks [[6]](#source-6).
- **Adds flexibility work.** Stretching for the front of the thigh, which is often tight in these kids [[2]](#source-2) [[7]](#source-7).
- **Brings sport back gradually.** Training first, then games. In the Danish study, most kids were back within a year [[6]](#source-6).

Pain at night or at rest, a hot or swollen knee, pain that started suddenly after a hard jump or landing, limping without a clear reason or pain in the hip are reasons to see a doctor to rule out other problems.

## What a physical therapist adds

A physical therapist can:

- Confirm that the pain comes from the growth area below the kneecap and not from the kneecap joint, the tendon itself or the hip
- Measure strength and flexibility and watch how your child runs, jumps and lands [[2]](#source-2) [[6]](#source-6)
- Set up an activity plan that keeps your child as active as possible while the pain settles [[6]](#source-6)
- Build a progressive strengthening and stretching program and move it forward as they improve [[6]](#source-6) [[7]](#source-7)
- Work with parents and coaches on training load and a step-by-step return to sport
- Refer you to a doctor if anything in the exam suggests a different problem

## The takeaway

- Osgood-Schlatter disease is an irritation of the growth area below the kneecap and affects about 1 in 10 adolescents.
- It is far more common in kids who play sports, and it usually starts around the growth spurt.
- Most cases settle when growth ends, but it can limit sport for months, and some people still have pain years later.
- An active plan of load management, strengthening and a gradual return to sport was linked with good results in a Danish study, though better trials are needed.
- Surgery is rarely needed and is mainly for adults with lasting symptoms.
