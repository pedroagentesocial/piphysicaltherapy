You roll over in bed, lie back or look up at a high shelf, and the room starts to spin. It stops after a short while, then comes back the next time you move your head that way. That is the classic pattern of **benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV)**, an inner ear disorder defined by repeated episodes of vertigo brought on by changes in head position [[1]](#source-1) [[17]](#source-17).

"Benign" means it is not dangerous in itself. But it can be miserable, and it can make you fall. **If your dizziness comes with other symptoms such as weakness, trouble speaking or vision changes, go straight to the warning signs at the end of this article.**

## What causes it

Your inner ear contains tiny calcium crystals that help you sense movement. In BPPV, some of them come loose and drift into one of the fluid-filled canals that sense head rotation, so moving your head sends the wrong signal to your brain [[3]](#source-3). It can follow a head injury, or develop just from getting older [[3]](#source-3).

There are three canals on each side, and the crystals can end up in any of them. The back (posterior) canal is the most common [[18]](#source-18), but the shares vary a lot between studies [[18]](#source-18) [[4]](#source-4). Which canal is involved decides which maneuver will work, so the diagnosis matters.

## How common is it?

In a German population study, 2.4% of adults had BPPV at some point in their lives, and it was about twice as common in women (3.2%) as in men (1.6%) [[2]](#source-2). It becomes much more common with age: by 80, almost 10% of people had experienced it [[2]](#source-2).

It is also undertreated. In that study, 86% of people with BPPV saw a doctor, had to interrupt daily activities or took sick leave, but only 8% received an effective treatment [[2]](#source-2).

## How it is diagnosed

The US clinical guideline recommends the **Dix-Hallpike test**: you are moved from sitting to lying back with your head turned to one side, and the clinician watches for a typical pattern of eye movements [[1]](#source-1). If that test does not show the classic pattern, a roll test checks the side canals [[1]](#source-1).

When the test is clear and nothing else seems unusual, the guideline recommends **against** routine brain imaging and vestibular testing [[1]](#source-1). It also recommends checking whether something else could be causing the dizziness [[1]](#source-1).

## How it is treated

**A repositioning maneuver is the treatment of choice.** The guideline strongly recommends treating BPPV of the back canal with a canalith repositioning procedure, such as the Epley maneuver, or referring you to someone who can [[1]](#source-1). It is a series of slow head and body positions that guides the loose crystals out of the canal [[17]](#source-17).

Another Cochrane review found that the Epley maneuver alone works in just under 80% of people, as measured by a negative Dix-Hallpike test afterward [[6]](#source-6). In many cases one session is enough, while other people need it several times [[3]](#source-3). There were no serious side effects in the trials, although 17% to 32% of people felt nauseated during the maneuver, and some could not tolerate it because of neck problems [[5]](#source-5).

What else the evidence says:

- **No special restrictions afterward.** The guideline strongly recommends against routine restrictions on posture or movement after the maneuver [[1]](#source-1). A Cochrane review found that restrictions did not improve symptoms [[6]](#source-6).
- **Medicines are not the answer.** The guideline recommends against routine use of drugs that suppress the inner ear, such as antihistamines or benzodiazepines [[1]](#source-1), and they often do not work well for vertigo [[17]](#source-17).
- **Home exercises are slower.** A single Epley maneuver worked better than a week of Brandt-Daroff exercises done three times a day [[5]](#source-5), and home exercises may take longer to work [[17]](#source-17).
- **Self-treatment can work, if it is the right one.** In one trial, 95% of people who did a modified Epley at home were free of positional vertigo after a week [[7]](#source-7). In another, people with a recurrence did better when their home maneuver matched the canal involved (72% vs. 43% resolved) [[8]](#source-8).
- **It may go away on its own.** Episodes lasted a median of 2 weeks in the population study [[2]](#source-2), and in untreated patients, back canal BPPV took an average of 39 days to clear [[4]](#source-4). Waiting with follow-up is an option, but the maneuver speeds things up [[1]](#source-1) [[5]](#source-5).

The guideline recommends a recheck within a month, and a closer look for other inner ear or brain problems if symptoms persist [[1]](#source-1). Vestibular rehabilitation with a trained clinician is another option [[1]](#source-1).

## Will it come back?

Often, yes, but it is usually treatable again [[17]](#source-17).

| Study | People | Follow-up | Came back |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nunez, 2000 [[9]](#source-9) | 168 | Average of 26 months | 26.8%, estimated at about 15% per year |
| Pérez, 2012 [[10]](#source-10) | 69 | Average of 47 months | 27%, half of them within 6 months |
| Kim, 2023 [[8]](#source-8) | 585 | At least 2 years | 21.9% |

These studies used different methods, so the rates vary. In a Korean trial of 1,050 people with BPPV, those with low vitamin D levels who took vitamin D and calcium had fewer recurrences (0.83 vs. 1.10 per person per year) [[11]](#source-11). The trial was not placebo controlled, and supplements were given only to people whose blood test showed a low level, so ask your doctor before starting any [[11]](#source-11).

## Dizziness and falls

This is the biggest practical risk, especially for older adults. The guideline recommends checking for fall risk, balance and mobility problems and lack of support at home [[1]](#source-1). In one geriatric clinic, 9% of patients had BPPV no one had recognized, and they were more likely to have fallen in the previous 3 months [[12]](#source-12). More than 1 in 4 older adults fall each year, and falling once doubles the chance of falling again [[13]](#source-13).

Simple safety steps while you have symptoms [[16]](#source-16):

- Get out of bed slowly and sit on the edge for a while before you stand
- Sit down straight away when you feel dizzy
- Turn on the lights if you get up at night
- Move your head slowly and carefully, and squat instead of bending over to pick things up
- Use a walking stick if you are at risk of falling

## Warning signs: when dizziness is an emergency

Stroke is an uncommon cause of dizziness, but it is real. In emergency department patients over 44 with dizziness or imbalance, 3.2% had a stroke or mini-stroke; with dizziness alone and no other symptoms, it was 0.7% [[14]](#source-14).

**Call 911 right away** for sudden dizziness or loss of balance together with any of these [[15]](#source-15) [[16]](#source-16):

- Numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg, especially on one side
- Trouble speaking or understanding speech, or confusion
- Double vision, loss of vision or trouble seeing
- Trouble walking or lack of coordination
- A sudden severe headache with no known cause
- Hearing loss

The CDC’s B.E. F.A.S.T. reminder: **B**alance loss, **E**yes (vision changes), **F**ace drooping, **A**rm drifting down, **S**peech slurred or strange, **T**ime to call 911 [[15]](#source-15).

Get urgent medical advice if vertigo comes with a severe headache, vomiting or feeling very sick, or a very high temperature [[16]](#source-16).

## The takeaway

- BPPV causes short bursts of spinning when you change head position, and it gets more common with age.
- A simple repositioning maneuver is the recommended treatment and often works in one or a few sessions.
- Medicines and routine imaging usually are not needed, and you do not need special restrictions afterward.
- It often comes back, but it can be treated again, so knowing which canal is involved helps.
- Dizziness with weakness, trouble speaking or vision changes is an emergency: call 911.
