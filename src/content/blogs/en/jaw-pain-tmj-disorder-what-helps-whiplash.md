The temporomandibular joints (TMJs) connect your lower jaw to your skull, just in front of each ear. Temporomandibular disorders, or TMD, is the name for a group of problems that cause pain in these joints and in the chewing muscles. Pain in the muscles or the joint is the most common symptom. It can spread to the face or neck, and it can come with jaw stiffness, limited opening or locking, painful clicking or grating, and changes in how your teeth fit together [[1]](#source-1).

TMD affects about 5% of US adults, and it is about twice as common in women, especially between ages 35 and 44 [[1]](#source-1). Worldwide estimates are a little higher, at 6% to 9% of adults, which makes it the second most common long-lasting musculoskeletal pain problem after low back pain [[2]](#source-2).

The good news: many TMDs last only a short time and go away on their own [[1]](#source-1).

## What counts as TMD

Clinicians use standard diagnostic criteria that separate pain in the chewing muscles, pain in the joint itself, problems with the small disc inside the joint, joint wear, and headaches caused by TMD [[3]](#source-3). Knowing which one you have helps guide treatment.

**Clicking or popping without pain is common and normal, and it does not need treatment** [[1]](#source-1).

## What causes it

In most cases the exact cause is not clear. Injury to the joint can lead to some TMDs, and genes, stress and how a person processes pain may also play a role [[1]](#source-1). The NHS lists teeth grinding, wear and tear of the joint, a blow to the head or face, and stress among the causes [[4]](#source-4).

A common belief is that a bad bite causes TMD. Research does not support the idea that a bad bite or orthodontic treatment such as braces causes it [[1]](#source-1).

## Whiplash and the jaw

People with jaw pain after a car accident often wonder whether the two are connected. The research gives a mixed answer.

Whiplash was much more common in the history of people with TMD, and people who had both reported more jaw pain, more limited opening, more headaches and more stress symptoms than people whose TMD was limited to the face [[5]](#source-5). The authors suggested whiplash might trigger or worsen TMD in some people [[5]](#source-5).

But when researchers followed people forward from the crash, the picture changed. A Danish study examined 19 people within 4 weeks of a rear-end collision and compared them with 20 people who had sprained an ankle. Jaw pain was rare in both groups, and there was no meaningful difference in jaw symptoms or exam findings at 4 weeks or at 6 months [[6]](#source-6).

Taken together: most people with whiplash do not develop TMD, but when jaw and neck problems occur together, they tend to be more stubborn, and both deserve attention.

## What helps

Start with simple self-care [[1]](#source-1) [[4]](#source-4):

- Eat soft foods for a while, such as pasta, omelets and soup
- Use heat or cold, whichever feels better, along with gentle jaw stretches and exercises
- Try over-the-counter pain relievers such as ibuprofen for short periods
- Avoid chewing gum, biting your nails or pen tops, and biting food with your front teeth
- Notice and ease off clenching, and avoid yawning too wide
- Find ways to relax, since stress may play a role

For pain that has lasted 3 months or more, an international BMJ guideline panel reviewed the evidence and made these recommendations [[2]](#source-2):

| Recommendation | Treatments |
| --- | --- |
| Strongly in favor | Cognitive behavioral therapy (with or without biofeedback or relaxation), therapist-assisted jaw mobilization, manual trigger point therapy, supervised postural exercise, supervised jaw exercise and stretching, and usual care such as home exercises, education and reassurance |
| Conditionally in favor | Manipulation, jaw exercise with mobilization, acupuncture, and cognitive behavioral therapy combined with anti-inflammatories |
| Conditionally against | Removable bite splints, low-level laser, TENS, Botox, steroid and other injections, gabapentin, and several others |
| Strongly against | Splints that permanently change your bite, surgery to remove the joint disc, and anti-inflammatories combined with opioids |

These recommendations apply to long-lasting pain, not to TMD that started recently [[2]](#source-2).

Not every review is as confident. An earlier review of 48 trials of manual therapy and exercise rated the evidence as low quality and found no clear sign that exercise beats other conservative treatments, although manual therapy, alone or combined with exercises for the jaw or neck, looked promising [[7]](#source-7). The honest summary is that hands-on care and exercise are reasonable, low-risk first steps, not guaranteed cures.

## What to be careful with

The NIH advises trying simple, reversible treatments first [[1]](#source-1):

- **Treatments that permanently change your teeth or bite**, such as crowns, grinding down teeth or orthodontics, do not have enough evidence behind them and may make the problem worse.
- **Night guards and splints** have limited evidence. They should not permanently change your bite, and you should stop using one if it causes pain.
- **Surgery, arthroscopy and joint implants** are permanent and are generally reserved for joint damage that cannot be managed any other way or severe symptoms that persist despite other treatments.

Before any irreversible procedure, ask about the risks and consider a second opinion, ideally from an orofacial pain specialist [[1]](#source-1).

## When to get medical help

**Call 911** if jaw pain comes with signs of a heart attack, such as shortness of breath, sweating, nausea or vomiting [[8]](#source-8).

**Get prompt medical advice** if you cannot eat or drink, have trouble opening your mouth fully or your jaw is locking, or have frequent severe headaches, pain or tenderness at the side of your head or scalp, or vision problems such as double vision [[4]](#source-4).

## The takeaway

- TMD is common, especially in women, and many cases go away on their own.
- Painless clicking is normal and does not need treatment.
- Self-care, jaw exercises, hands-on therapy and help with stress are the recommended first steps for long-lasting jaw pain.
- Avoid treatments that permanently change your bite or teeth.
- Whiplash does not cause TMD in most people, but jaw and neck pain that occur together are worth treating together.
