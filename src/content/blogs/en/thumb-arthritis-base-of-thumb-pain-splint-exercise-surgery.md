Turning a key. Opening a jar. Pulling up a zipper, writing a grocery list, pinching a bag clip shut. When the base of the thumb hurts, everyday tasks that used to take no thought start to hurt.

The most common cause is arthritis in the joint where the thumb meets the wrist. It is very common, it is not dangerous, and most people can manage it without surgery [[1]](#source-1). Here is what the research says about the options.

## What thumb arthritis is

The joint at the base of the thumb, called the carpometacarpal or CMC joint, lets the thumb swivel, pivot and pinch. That freedom of movement also makes it one of the joints most likely to develop arthritis as people age [[1]](#source-1). Over time, the smooth cartilage that covers the ends of the bones wears down and the bones start to rub [[1]](#source-1).

Typical symptoms include [[1]](#source-1):

- Pain at the base of the thumb when gripping, pinching or twisting, such as turning a key or opening a jar
- Aching after using the thumb for a long time, and sometimes pain at night
- A weak grip or pinch
- Swelling, tenderness or a bony bump at the base of the thumb
- Less movement in the thumb

A doctor can usually diagnose it with an exam and X-rays. Many people with thumb arthritis also have carpal tunnel syndrome, so that may be checked too [[1]](#source-1).

## Who gets it

Women are 2 to 3 times more likely than men to develop it, and it becomes more common after 50. A family history, being overweight, a past thumb or wrist injury, loose joints, smoking and work or hobbies with a lot of gripping and pinching also raise the risk [[1]](#source-1).

In a Finnish population study of 3,595 adults aged 30 and older, X-rays showed thumb-base arthritis in 7% of men and 15% of women, and higher body weight went with higher rates [[2]](#source-2). With age it becomes almost universal on X-rays: among 615 people with a wrist fracture, signs of thumb-base arthritis were present in 91% of those older than 80 [[3]](#source-3).

X-rays do not tell the whole story, though. Many people have arthritis on X-rays and no symptoms, and some people whose thumbs hurt find that the pain fades over time, with or without treatment [[1]](#source-1).

## Splints: the treatment with the strongest support

A splint that supports the base of the thumb can ease pain during activities or overnight [[1]](#source-1). The 2019 guideline from the American College of Rheumatology and the Arthritis Foundation strongly recommends a hand orthosis for arthritis at the base of the thumb [[4]](#source-4).

The splint may take time to pay off. In a French trial of 112 people, most of them women, a custom-made splint worn at night made no difference to pain after 1 month. After 12 months, however, pain and hand disability had improved clearly more in the splint group [[6]](#source-6):

At 12 months, 86% of the splint group were still wearing it more than 5 nights a week, and no side effects were reported [[6]](#source-6). Neither the patients nor the assessors were blinded, which is hard to avoid with a splint [[6]](#source-6).

## Hand exercises

Gentle exercises can improve thumb motion and strengthen the muscles that support the joint, and a hand therapist can guide them [[1]](#source-1). A Cochrane review of hand osteoarthritis pooled five trials with 381 people and found that exercise reduced pain by about half a point on a 0 to 10 scale, with similar small gains in function and finger stiffness [[5]](#source-5). The evidence was low quality, the benefits were not clearly sustained at follow-up of 6 to 12 months, and the few side effects reported were mild flares of pain or joint inflammation [[5]](#source-5).

In other words, exercise helps a little on its own. It seems to work best as part of a plan.

## Putting treatments together

That is what an Australian trial tested. It randomly assigned 204 people with thumb-base arthritis, average age 66, to either education on self-management and joint-friendly ways of using the hand, or the same education plus a thumb splint, hand exercises and diclofenac gel [[7]](#source-7). After 6 weeks, hand function improved more in the combined group, and by 12 weeks pain had also dropped more [[7]](#source-7). There were 34 minor side effects in the combined group, mostly skin reactions and pain from the exercises [[7]](#source-7).

Small changes in how you use your hand count too. Avoiding the activities and tools that make symptoms worse can ease thumb pain, and ice for 5 to 15 minutes several times a day can help with pain and swelling [[1]](#source-1).

## Gels, pills and injections

Anti-inflammatory medicines such as ibuprofen or naproxen can help with pain. A gel such as diclofenac, rubbed onto the joint, is another option and may cause fewer side effects than pills [[1]](#source-1). The American College of Rheumatology conditionally recommends topical anti-inflammatory gels for hand arthritis [[4]](#source-4).

A steroid shot into the joint can bring relief for some people, but the effect may wear off [[1]](#source-1). The guideline conditionally recommends it for hand arthritis [[4]](#source-4), but evidence for the thumb is mixed: in a small trial of 40 people, a steroid injection did no better than an injection of salt water at 24 weeks [[8]](#source-8).

Newer options are being studied. In a French trial of 60 people who also wore a rigid splint, a botulinum toxin injection reduced pain more than a salt water injection at 3 months, but 47% of those who received it had temporary weakness in the muscles at the base of the thumb [[9]](#source-9). It is not a standard treatment.

## When surgery is considered

If other treatments no longer control the pain, surgery is an option, usually as an outpatient procedure [[1]](#source-1). The most common operation in the US removes a small wrist bone at the base of the thumb, sometimes using a tendon to support or cushion the thumb. Other options include fusing the joint, which gives a strong pinch but less motion, or replacing it with an implant [[1]](#source-1).

A 2026 Cochrane review of 25 trials with 1,591 people found no clear evidence that any one operation works better than the others. For example, adding a tendon reconstruction to removal of the bone made little or no difference to pain or function [[10]](#source-10). It found no trials comparing surgery with non-surgical care [[10]](#source-10).

After surgery, expect a cast or splint for 4 to 8 weeks and several months of recovery, with grip strength improving for up to 9 to 12 months. A hand therapist helps restore movement and strength [[1]](#source-1).

## When to see a clinician

**Get it checked** if pain at the base of the thumb keeps coming back, makes gripping or pinching hard, or comes with swelling or a bump [[1]](#source-1).

**Also mention** numbness or tingling in the fingers, since carpal tunnel syndrome often appears alongside thumb arthritis [[1]](#source-1).

## The takeaway

- Thumb arthritis is very common, especially in women after 50, and it is not dangerous.
- Symptoms do not always match the X-ray, and pain sometimes eases over time.
- A thumb splint is the best-supported treatment. In one trial, night splinting clearly reduced pain after a year.
- Hand exercises help a little on their own, and a combined plan of education, a splint, exercises and gel worked better than education alone.
- Steroid injections give mixed results. Surgery is an option when other treatments stop working, and no single operation has proven better than the others.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If the base of your thumb hurts when you grip or pinch, an evaluation can confirm the cause and build a plan with exercises, splinting advice and easier ways to get through your day.
