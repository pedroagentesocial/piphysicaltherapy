Despite the name, you do not need a racket to get tennis elbow. It is pain on the outside of the elbow, at the point where the forearm muscles that lift your wrist attach to the bone. About **1.3% of adults** had it in a Finnish population study, most often between the ages of 45 and 54 [[1]](#source-1). In a US study of diagnosed cases, the people most often affected were office workers and health care workers [[2]](#source-2), and the physical therapy guideline notes that many people with it do not play racket sports [[3]](#source-3).

It has been linked to handling tools heavier than about 2 pounds (1 kg), and to repetitive arm movements for more than 2 hours a day [[4]](#source-4). And although it is often described as inflammation, the tendon usually shows degeneration or small tears by the time people seek care [[3]](#source-3) [[14]](#source-14).

## How long does it last?

This is the good news: most people get better, but it takes time. A review of 24 trials looked at about 1,000 people who received no active treatment. Symptoms fell by about half every 3 to 4 months, and **about 9 in 10 people had improved at one year** [[5]](#source-5).

"Improved" does not always mean symptom-free, though, and not everyone follows that pattern. The physical therapy guideline notes that many people seeking care still report symptoms after a year [[3]](#source-3), and the NHS says it can sometimes last more than a year [[15]](#source-15).

## What the trials show

In a well-known Australian trial, people with tennis elbow were assigned to physical therapy (8 sessions of elbow manipulation and exercise), a steroid injection, or wait and see [[6]](#source-6):

| Share who were "much improved" or "completely recovered" | Wait and see | Steroid injection | Physical therapy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6 weeks | 27% | 78% | 65% |
| 26 weeks | 83% | 45% | 86% |
| 52 weeks | 90% | 68% | 94% |

*Source: Bisset et al., BMJ, 2006 [[6]](#source-6).*

A later placebo-controlled trial in *JAMA* confirmed it: at one year, a steroid injection led to worse outcomes than a placebo injection (83% vs. 96% recovered or much improved) and many more recurrences (54% vs. 12%) [[7]](#source-7).

What about physical therapy? The honest answer is that it seems to **speed up recovery rather than change where you end up**. In the *JAMA* trial, physical therapy gave a clear early benefit, with 39% vs. 10% improved at 4 weeks among people who did not get a steroid injection, but there was no significant difference at one year [[7]](#source-7). A review of 30 trials found low-certainty evidence that exercise beats passive treatments, and the effect is small [[8]](#source-8). For many people, getting better weeks sooner is still well worth it, especially if the pain affects your work.

## What the guidelines recommend

The physical therapy guideline’s main recommendations, rated moderate evidence, are [[3]](#source-3):

- **Resistance exercises for the wrist extensors**, in any form (isometric, concentric or eccentric), on their own or combined with other treatment such as hands-on therapy
- **Elbow joint mobilization or manipulation** for short-term pain relief and pain-free grip
- **Rigid taping** for immediate, short-term relief when the elbow is very irritable

A UK guideline agrees that people should be offered physical therapy, along with advice on self-management, and says steroid injections and shockwave therapy should not be used [[9]](#source-9). Guidelines do not always agree: the physical therapy guideline supports dry needling [[3]](#source-3), while the UK guideline says there is no evidence it beats a placebo [[9]](#source-9).

## Braces, injections and surgery

- **Forearm straps and braces:** evidence for intermediate or long-term relief is conflicting. A strap worn during activity may help with immediate pain, based on expert opinion [[3]](#source-3).
- **PRP and blood injections:** a Cochrane review of 32 studies found they probably provide little or no clinically important benefit over placebo. Success rates were 67% with injection and 65% with placebo at 3 months [[10]](#source-10).
- **Shockwave therapy:** a Cochrane review found little or no benefit for pain and function [[11]](#source-11).
- **Surgery:** a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to support or refute it [[12]](#source-12), and a later review of poor-quality studies found it was no more effective than non-surgical or sham treatment [[13]](#source-13). Surgery is usually considered only if symptoms do not respond to 6 to 12 months of non-surgical care [[14]](#source-14).

## What you can do now

- Reduce or change the activities that make it worse for a few weeks, rather than stopping everything [[14]](#source-14) [[15]](#source-15)
- Use a hot or cold pack wrapped in a towel for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours [[15]](#source-15)
- Ask about an anti-inflammatory gel, which may help with pain for up to 4 weeks [[9]](#source-9) [[15]](#source-15)
- Look at your setup at work: guideline advice includes ergonomic changes and workstation adjustments [[3]](#source-3)

See a clinician if you still have pain after at least 2 weeks of self-care [[15]](#source-15). Arm pain can also come from the neck or from nerve compression, which can feel similar, so a proper exam matters [[14]](#source-14).

**Get urgent care** if you have pain or trouble moving your elbow after a fall or injury, a visibly deformed elbow, significant swelling, redness or heat around the elbow with a fever, or a sudden loss of strength or feeling in the arm [[16]](#source-16).

## The takeaway

- Tennis elbow is not just for tennis players, and it is linked to repetitive and forceful work.
- About 9 in 10 people improve within a year, though recovery can be slow and is not guaranteed.
- Steroid injections help at first but lead to worse results and more relapses over time.
- Physical therapy and resistance exercise help you improve sooner, even if the long-term difference is small.
- PRP, shockwave and surgery have not been shown to beat placebo or sham treatment.

You can start physical therapy in Utah without a referral. If elbow pain is getting in the way of your work, an evaluation can confirm the cause and build a plan that fits your job.
